# AI chatbots and student learning in physics and quantitative courses (evidence as of October 2026)

*Research notes written by Claude (Opus 5.5), 2026-10-25, for a report writer. Every claim carries a source link; preprints are flagged.*

## 1. How many students use generative AI for coursework, and for what?

### Takeaway
Use is close to universal among UK undergraduates (95% in Dec 2025) and a large majority of US undergraduates (85% in 2025), and it's growing fast among US teens (13% → 26% → about 54% between 2023 and fall 2025). Most reported use is "tutor-like" (explanations, brainstorming, studying), but a growing minority hands in AI output directly. I found no survey that breaks out physics or STEM majors with a usable figure.

### Cited Findings
**UK (HEPI / Kortext, Savanta fieldwork)**
- HEPI Student Generative AI Survey 2026 (HEPI Report 199, published 12 March 2026; 1,054 full-time UK undergraduates; fieldwork December 2025): 95% use AI in at least one way; 94% use it to help with assessed work; 12% include AI-generated text directly in assessed work, up from 8% (2025) and 3% (2024) — [HEPI 2026 report page](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/reports/student-generative-ai-survey-2026/); [PDF](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/HEPI-Report-199-Gen-AI-Survey-2026.pdf)
- Same survey: 65% say assessment at their institution has "changed significantly" in response to AI; 49% think AI has improved their student experience; only 36% feel encouraged by their institution to use AI; 39% of Russell Group students say their institution encourages AI use (up from 26% the year before) — [HEPI 2026](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/reports/student-generative-ai-survey-2026/)
- The HEPI landing page gives no STEM-vs-other breakdown (it notes only that Arts and Humanities students feel particularly under-supported) — [HEPI 2026](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/reports/student-generative-ai-survey-2026/)

**US undergraduates**
- Inside Higher Ed Student Voice survey 2025 (1,047 students at 166 two- and four-year institutions; reported Aug 29, 2025): 85% used AI for coursework in the past year (86% at four-year, 79% at community colleges). Top uses: brainstorming 55%, asking questions "like a tutor" 50%, studying for exams/quizzes 46%, editing writing 44%, advanced search 42% — [eWeek summary of Inside Higher Ed Student Voice 2025](https://www.eweek.com/fr/news/student-voice-survey-2025/)
- Same survey, on learning: among past-year users, 55% reported mixed effects ("useful at times, but made me think less deeply"), 27% positive, 7% negative. Only 21% favored AI-detection software and 18% supported classroom bans — [eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/fr/news/student-voice-survey-2025/)
- A secondary report of the same Student Voice data says 25% used AI to complete assignments and 19% to write full essays — [The Implicator, May 18, 2026](https://www.implicator.ai/universities-return-to-blue-books-and-proctors-as-ai-detectors-prove-unreliable.md) (secondary source; I didn't verify these two figures against Inside Higher Ed directly)
- Tyton Partners, *Time for Class 2025* (published June 11, 2025; 3,300+ students, instructors, and administrators at 900+ US institutions; focus on introductory and developmental courses): about 42% of students and 30% of instructors use generative AI weekly or more — [Tyton Partners](https://tytonpartners.com/time-for-class-2025/). As reported secondhand: 71% of instructors spend time monitoring cheating, 61% have redesigned assessments, and only 28% of institutions have formal institution-wide GenAI policies — [The Implicator](https://www.implicator.ai/universities-return-to-blue-books-and-proctors-as-ai-detectors-prove-unreliable.md)
- EDUCAUSE 2025 Students and Technology Report (nearly 40,000 US undergraduates at 160+ institutions; fielded Sept–Dec 2024; weighted): about 43% had *not* used AI for courses; among users, common uses were brainstorming 33%, refining ideas 24%, organizing 24%; 52% say most instructors prohibit AI use; only 51% have received guidance on AI use — [Poets&Quants summary of EDUCAUSE 2025](https://poetsandquantsforundergrads.com/news/students-demand-more-than-just-wi-fi-report-highlights-gaps-in-tech-integration-career-readiness/)
- Note the conflict: EDUCAUSE (fall 2024, very large weighted sample) finds ~57% had used AI for courses, while Inside Higher Ed (2025, ~1,000 students) finds 85%. The difference is plausibly timing, question wording, and sampling; not reconcilable from the summaries.

**US teens (Pew Research Center)**
- Pew (survey Sept 18 – Oct 10, 2024; 1,391 teens 13–17, Ipsos KnowledgePanel, weighted): 26% had used ChatGPT for schoolwork, double the 13% in 2023. Acceptability: researching new topics 54% acceptable / 9% not; **solving math problems 29% acceptable / 28% not**; writing essays 18% / 42% — [Pew Research, Jan 15, 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork-double-the-share-in-2023)
- Pew (survey Sept 25 – Oct 9, 2025): about 54% of teens use AI chatbots (any, not just ChatGPT) for schoolwork; about 1 in 10 do "all or most" of their schoolwork with AI help; solving math problems is named among common uses; 59% think cheating with AI has become a regular part of student life — [GovTech summary of Pew 2025](https://www.govtech.com/education/k-12/survey-finds-54-of-u-s-teens-use-ai-for-schoolwork). (2024 and 2025 figures ask about different things: ChatGPT specifically vs any AI chatbot.)

**Physics-specific usage**
- Michigan State online calculus-based intro physics course (data from about a year after ChatGPT's release): "students still mostly rely on traditional internet resources," with little evidence of a broad shift to AI; help-seeking patterns strongly affected unsupervised low-stakes quiz scores but had non-significant effects on supervised assessments — Kortemeyer & Bauer, *Phys. Rev. Phys. Educ. Res.* 20, 010145 (2024), DOI 10.1103/PhysRevPhysEducRes.20.010145 — [arXiv:2312.15334](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2312.15334); [Astrobites summary](https://astrobites.org/2024/12/02/cheat-sites-amp-ai-amp-large-language-models-oh-my/) (peer-reviewed; data are now dated)

### Inferences
- The relevant population (intro physics students in 2026) almost certainly has AI-assisted homework as a default option; the 2023 MSU finding of "mostly traditional internet resources" is probably obsolete given the jump in every survey since.
- Teens are notably more ambivalent about AI for *math problems* (29% vs 28%) than for research — the quantitative case is exactly where norms are least settled.

### Gaps
- No 2025–2026 survey I found gives a usable physics- or STEM-major breakdown of AI use rates or types of use.
- I didn't retrieve the HEPI 2026 PDF's subject-level tables (if any) or Tyton's 2026 edition (if one exists).

## 2. How well do current models do on physics assessments?

### Takeaway
In about three years models went from a barely passing intro-physics student (ChatGPT, Jan 2023) to top-of-class on text-based intro exams (GPT-5 Thinking, 2025: 96% and 89% on two semesters' finals) and to IPhO-gold-medal level on olympiad theory (an agent built on Gemini 2.5 Pro, 2025). The remaining weak spots are representation-heavy items (diagrams, graphs, right-hand rules, 3D perspective) and labs/vivas, and correct answers sometimes come from flawed reasoning.

### Cited Findings
**Trajectory on intro courses and the FCI**
- ChatGPT (Jan 9, 2023 release) worked through a calculus-based intro course at MSU: ~55% of 76 homework problems (avg 1.88 attempts; numerical errors in 25 of 51 numerical problems), FCI 18/30 (60%), first-semester final 14/30 (47%); it "would narrowly pass this course while exhibiting many of the preconceptions and errors of a beginning learner" — Kortemeyer, [arXiv:2301.12127](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12127) (v2 Feb 2, 2023; the arXiv page I saw lists no journal reference, though I believe it appeared in PRPER in 2023 — verify before citing as peer-reviewed)
- FCI, March 2023: ChatGPT-3.5 got 15/23 usable items (~65%; 50% counting excluded items wrong), around a class median; GPT-4 got 22/23, top decile of the author's post-test students, with stable answers under rephrasing. Neither could convincingly simulate a novice when asked — West, "AI and the FCI," [arXiv:2303.01067](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01067) (preprint; journal status not confirmed)
- GPT-4 "could accurately solve the FCI"; asking it to mimic a different student cohort produced no variation, but assigning it a specific preconception produced student-like variation — Kieser, Wulff, Kuhn & Küchemann, *Phys. Rev. Phys. Educ. Res.* 19, 020150 (2023), DOI 10.1103/PhysRevPhysEducRes.19.020150 — [LMU open-access record](https://oa-fund.ub.uni-muenchen.de/id/eprint/1058) (peer-reviewed; Tufino et al. cite it as GPT-4 "over 80% correctness" on the FCI)
- GPT-5 Thinking (2025): TUG-K v4.0 graph-interpretation inventory 24/26 (92.3%; both misses were matching graphs to integrals); first-semester final 27/30 (96.4% under course rules), second-semester final 25/30 (89.3%), errors clustered on circuit diagrams and ruler-based ray diagrams; 100% favorable "expert-like" answers on the CLASS attitudes survey across five runs; the GPT series went "from D-student to A-student" in about two years — Kortemeyer, "The Boiling-Frog Problem of Physics Education," [arXiv:2508.08842](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.08842); published in *The Physics Teacher* Jan 1, 2026, DOI 10.1119/5.0296601 — [Newswise press release](https://www.newswise.com/articles/when-is-it-time-to-jump-the-boiling-frog-problem-of-ai-use-in-physics-education)
- Multimodal AI "can now solve introductory physics problems at roughly the level of top-scoring students" — Babayeva, Widenhorn & Kortemeyer, "When AI gets an A: Introductory physics education when answers are cheap," *Europhysics News* 57(2), 16–19 (online May 29, 2026), DOI 10.1051/epn/2026210 — [EPN](https://www.europhysicsnews.org/10.1051/epn/2026210) (magazine article, not a research paper)

**A contamination-resistant inventory (2026)**
- On a new Classical Relativity Concept Inventory (21 items, 30 runs each; data Dec 2025–Jan 2026): Gemini 3 Flash 97.0%, Gemini 3 Pro 88.6%, GPT-5.2 73.3% with reasoning off (about 85–86% with reasoning on), students (N = 267) 61.5%. Dominant failure: visual misinterpretation (3D perspective, inclined planes, reference-frame diagrams); GPT-5.2 scored 0% on one item purely from a misread diagram. Also "false positives": correct final answers from flawed reasoning or visual descriptions. When wrong, models converge on one distractor, while students spread errors. The authors argue FCI scores may be inflated by decades of public exposure of the items — Tufino, Giovanzana, Zamboni, Onorato & Oss, [arXiv:2605.09602](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09602) (appears to be an unpublished manuscript)
- Doctoral thesis (Uppsala, defended June 9, 2026; supervisor Bor Gregorcic): vision-language models on research-based inventories (kinematics, E&M) often performed at or above typical student levels, but "uneven and fragile": they could state plausible physics strategies while misreading the representation; common errors in graph interpretation, spatial coordination, and **right-hand-rule procedures**; strong scores "don't show that the models understand physics in the educational sense," and models shouldn't be treated as reliable tutors or assessment supports for representation-rich tasks without local validation — Polverini thesis defense, [Uppsala University](https://www.uu.se/en/events/defences/2026-06-09-giulia-polverini-vision-language-model-performance-on-physics-tasks-exploring-ai-capabilities-on-research-based-assessments-involving-visual-representations)

**Whole degree and olympiad**
- GPT-4 (prompted Nov 2023–Feb 2024, "maximal cheating" approach) across a full UK BSc Physics program would earn about 65% (a 2:1) on coursework but fails the degree because it can't pass compulsory lab elements and the final project viva; strong on coding, single-step problems, and multiple choice; weak on multi-step problems, complex diagrams, and deep-insight questions — Pimbblet & Morrell, "Can ChatGPT pass a physics degree?", [arXiv:2412.01312](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01312) (published in *European Journal of Physics* per the paper's acknowledgments; volume not confirmed)
- IPhO 2025 theory (out of 30): the "Physics Supernova" agent (built on Gemini 2.5 Pro, with image-analysis and answer-review tools) scored 23.5 ± 0.8 over 5 runs vs a gold-medalist median of 22.8, ranking 14th of 406 contestants; Gemini 2.5 Pro alone scored 21.4 ± 1.1. Theory only, no experimental problems; graded by former medalists against official criteria — Qiu et al., [arXiv:2509.01659](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01659v1) (NeurIPS 2025 workshop/poster listing at [neurips.cc](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/122339))

### Inferences
- For a beginner's text-and-numbers problem set, a 2026 model is effectively an A student; "the student got the right answer" no longer signals understanding on unsupervised work.
- The residual model weaknesses (diagrams, directional/right-hand-rule reasoning, correct answers from bad reasoning) are exactly where a learner who leans on a chatbot would be misled without noticing. That's relevant to magnetism and rotation in particular.

### Gaps
- I didn't find a 2026 peer-reviewed study of frontier models on the standard FCI itself (most recent work moved to new or image-based inventories because of contamination).
- No source I found reports reliable error rates for models on typical *numerical* intro homework in 2026, beyond the lab-assistant note in Q4 below.

## 3. Evidence of harm

### Takeaway
The strongest causal evidence (a ~1,000-student field experiment in PNAS) shows that unguarded chatbot access during practice raises practice scores but lowers later unaided test scores (by 17%), while students overestimate how much they learned. A large 2025 university trial (working paper) finds course-integrated, answer-giving AI tutoring lowered final grades. Lab and observational studies point the same way: harm comes from *substituting* AI for the work, not from AI use per se.

### Cited Findings
- **Bastani et al. (PNAS 2025).** Pre-registered field experiment with nearly 1,000 Turkish high school math students (grades 9–11, 2023–24). Three arms: no AI; "GPT Base" (ChatGPT-like GPT-4 interface); "GPT Tutor" (prompted to give hints, not answers). During practice, GPT Base improved grades by 48% and GPT Tutor by 127%. On the subsequent exam without AI, GPT Base students did **17% worse** than controls; GPT Tutor students did about the same as controls (safeguards "largely mitigate" the harm). Students "used GPT-4 as a crutch" — Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı & Mariman, "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics," *PNAS* 122(26), e2422633122 (2025), DOI 10.1073/pnas.2422633122 — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html); grade levels from [Tech & Learning](https://www.techlearning.com/news/high-school-math-students-used-a-gpt-4-ai-tutor-they-did-worse); earlier SSRN version titled "Generative AI can harm learning" (2024) — [SSRN 4895486](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486)
- Same study, per Wharton's coverage: AI users were overly optimistic about how much they'd learned, high achievers included — [Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/). Note that the guarded tutor *didn't beat* the control on the unaided exam; it only avoided harm.
- **Liu et al. (University of Maryland, Oct 2026).** Randomized trial (instructors randomized within blocks of matched courses; Fall 2025; 2,379 undergraduates, 30 instructors, multiple disciplines including STEM) of a course-integrated GPT-4o tutor using retrieval over course materials, defaulting to direct explanation (only 2 instructors switched on the answer-withholding "tutor mode"; ~74% of student requests sought direct answers or explanations). In the exact-match subsample (1,353 students), final grades fell by 4.12 points (~0.37 SD) and letter grades by 0.11 GPA points, both significant; in the full sample the drop (2.88 points) was significant only at the 10% level. Homework scores showed no reliable effect; LMS participation fell ~0.90 SD — Liu et al., "The Effects of Course-Integrated AI Tutoring on Student Performance and Engagement: A Randomized University Trial," [IZA DP 18989](https://docs.iza.org/dp18989.pdf) (**working paper, not peer-reviewed**; very recent)
- **Substitution vs complementation.** Two pre-registered, incentivized lab experiments (coding) found no overall effect of LLM access on learning; exploratory analyses show students who used the LLM to *generate solutions* covered more topics but understood each less well, while those who used it for *explanations* improved understanding; LLMs widened the gap between low- and high-prior-knowledge students — Lehmann, Cornelius & Sting, "AI Meets the Classroom: When Do Large Language Models Harm Learning?", [arXiv:2409.09047](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047v2) (v2 March 2025; preprint)
- **Overtrust in physics.** Physics-background students solving physics exercises with ChatGPT (n = 27) vs a search engine (n = 12): nearly half of ChatGPT-supported solutions were wrongly assumed correct; 42% of ChatGPT queries were copy-paste vs 4% of search queries — Krupp et al., "Unreflected Acceptance," [arXiv:2309.03087](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2309.03087) (Aug 2023; preprint "under review"; small sample)
- **Homework decouples from learning.** In the MSU course, help-seeking patterns strongly influenced *unsupervised* quiz scores but not supervised exams; using the final exam as the learning measure, there was no significant difference between usage clusters — Kortemeyer & Bauer, PRPER 20, 010145 (2024) — [arXiv:2312.15334](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2312.15334); [Astrobites](https://astrobites.org/2024/12/02/cheat-sites-amp-ai-amp-large-language-models-oh-my/)
- **Cognitive engagement (essay writing, not quantitative).** 54 participants in LLM, search, and no-tool groups over three sessions; EEG connectivity weakest with the LLM; LLM users had trouble quoting their own essays and reported lowest ownership; in a fourth session (only 18 participants) LLM-to-no-tool switchers showed reduced connectivity — Kosmyna et al., "Your Brain on ChatGPT," [arXiv:2506.08872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872v1) (June 2025, v2 Dec 2025; **preprint**, small and attrited sample, widely over-reported in press)
- **Students' own sense:** 55% of US student AI users say it's useful but makes them think less deeply — [eWeek / Inside Higher Ed Student Voice 2025](https://www.eweek.com/fr/news/student-voice-survey-2025/)

### Inferences
- Across designs, the common thread is: when the AI produces the answer, practice performance rises and unaided performance falls or stays flat, and students can't tell from the inside. That's the "faked through coursework" mechanism in measured form.
- The Maryland result suggests merely putting a course-aligned chatbot in the LMS, without answer withholding, can be net negative at scale; it's a working paper, so weight it below Bastani.

### Gaps
- No physics-specific RCT of *unguarded* chatbot use on later unaided performance turned up; the causal harm evidence is from math (Bastani), coding (Lehmann), and mixed university courses (Liu).
- Bastani's GPT-4 error rate on the problems and the exact GPT Tutor design (whether it was given teacher solutions) weren't confirmed from what I could fetch; check the PNAS full text before citing specifics.

## 4. Evidence of benefit from well-designed AI tutoring

### Takeaway
There's credible evidence that tightly scaffolded AI tutors can beat good instruction on immediate tests: the Harvard intro-physics crossover RCT (Scientific Reports, 2025) found learning gains more than double those of in-class active learning (effect ~0.63 SD, likely underestimated), and a World Bank RCT in Nigeria found 0.23–0.31 SD gains. But the successful designs lean heavily on expert-authored structure, including pre-written step-by-step solutions to keep the AI accurate, and measure only short-term outcomes. A widely cited meta-analysis claiming large benefits was retracted in 2026.

### Cited Findings
- **Kestin, Miller et al. (Harvard; Scientific Reports 2025).** Randomized crossover trial in an intro physics course for life-science majors (233 enrolled, 194 analyzed; two consecutive weeks: surface tension and fluid flow). AI-tutor group median post-test 4.5 vs 3.5 for in-class active learning; median gain more than double; Mann–Whitney p < 10⁻⁸; regression effect size 0.63 (authors call it an underestimate due to ceiling; quantile regression 0.73–1.3 SD). Higher engagement (4.1 vs 3.6) and motivation (3.4 vs 3.1). Median time 49 minutes vs 60 in class; time on task didn't correlate with scores. 83% rated the AI's explanations as good as or better than human instructors' — Kestin, Miller, Klales, Milbourne & Ponti, "AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: an RCT introducing a novel research-based design in an authentic educational setting," *Scientific Reports* 15, 17458 (June 3, 2025), DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6 — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)
- **How the Harvard tutor was built** (the key design facts): custom GPT-4 platform "PS2 Pal"; system prompt built around active engagement, cognitive load, and growth mindset; the system prompt alone "could not reliably scaffold multi-part problems," so the platform walked students through each part in sequence; **accuracy relied on pre-written, step-by-step solutions** rather than model-generated answers, to reduce hallucination — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)
- **Harvard authors' own limits:** one course, one institution, first exposure to mostly independent introductory topics; post-tests given immediately (no retention measure); results may depend on the videos, expert-written question-specific prompts, and structured framework; no claim AI will beat active learning for complex synthesis; authors advise against replacing in-person instruction and warn AI may act as a crutch — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)
- **Bastani's guarded "GPT Tutor"** (hints, not answers) avoided the harm of unguarded AI but didn't produce a measurable gain over no-AI controls on the unaided exam — [RePEc/PNAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html); [Tech & Learning](https://www.techlearning.com/news/high-school-math-students-used-a-gpt-4-ai-tutor-they-did-worse)
- **World Bank, Nigeria.** RCT of six weeks of after-school virtual tutoring with Microsoft Copilot (GPT-4) for first-year senior secondary students: 0.31 SD on the overall assessment, 0.23 SD on English (the main outcome) — De Simone et al., "From Chalkboards to Chatbots: Evaluating the Impact of Generative AI on Learning Outcomes in Nigeria," World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 11125 (2025) — [World Bank Open Knowledge](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/15e1ff08-15ae-4f7a-b2a8-d146e6c113ee) (**working paper**; English, not STEM; the program also included teacher-guided sessions per other coverage, not verified here)
- **Physics AI assistants in practice:** a GPT-based lab assistant at Portland State answered >85% of student queries correctly and helpfully, but numerical and measurement questions were more error-prone (code execution reduced but didn't eliminate calculation errors), and students sometimes lost trust after wrong guidance; ETH's "Ethel" homework-feedback tool, anchored to the problem text and an instructor's sample solution, was rated helpful and correct about three-quarters of the time — Babayeva, Widenhorn & Kortemeyer, *Europhysics News* 57(2) (2026) — [EPN](https://www.europhysicsnews.org/10.1051/epn/2026210)
- **Lab partner case study:** high school students using ChatGPT in an acoustic-levitation / speed-of-sound lab; AI could address some of students' questions and may help problem-solving "when used in a structured way," but the teacher remained essential and students needed training — Kilde-Westberg, Johansson & Enger, "Generative AI as a lab partner: A case study," *Phys. Rev. Phys. Educ. Res.* 21, 020119 (Aug 2025), DOI 10.1103/ggy1-3kjk — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/0c0fe949f48b4638b0ba8ed4a62bf385) (qualitative case study)
- **Retracted meta-analysis (caution):** Wang & Fan, "The effect of ChatGPT on students' learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking: insights from a meta-analysis," *Humanities & Social Sciences Communications* (May 2025), claimed g = 0.867 on learning performance from 51 studies; cited 500+ times; **retracted in May 2026** over "discrepancies in the meta-analysis" (inconsistent study counts, improper weighting, a retracted study included, poorly controlled small studies; critics estimated the real effect closer to 0.5) — [Plagiarism Today, July 15, 2026](https://www.plagiarismtoday.com/2026/07/15/study-claiming-ai-helps-students-learn-retracted/)

### Inferences
- The benefit evidence and the harm evidence are consistent with each other: the AI helps when it's fenced into an expert-designed sequence that makes the student do each step, and hurts when it hands over answers. The Harvard design needed human-written step-by-step solutions to be trustworthy, which is itself an argument that the checkable structure has to come from somewhere other than the model.
- None of the benefit studies measure retention weeks later, or transfer to unaided problem solving in a later course.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed 2026 replication of the Harvard result in another physics course or institution.
- Effects on delayed tests and on the weakest students under well-designed tutors remain unmeasured in the sources I found (Lehmann et al. suggest gaps widen under unstructured use).

## 5. How physics departments and teachers are changing assessment; AAPT/APS guidance

### Takeaway
The dominant move is back to supervised and live assessment: proctored in-person exams, oral defenses and short oral spot checks, process-graded paper or whiteboard work, labs, and AI-use disclosure, with unsupervised online closed-response work dropped as evidence of mastery. Physics-education leaders (notably Kortemeyer) argue for grading process over answers. I found **no formal AAPT or APS position statement** on generative AI in physics teaching; AAPT's activity is a TPT column and a PERC 2026 conference focused on AI, and APS's 2026 AI policy concerns journal authorship and reviewing.

### Cited Findings
**Physics-specific recommendations**
- Kortemeyer's recommendations (2025/2026): stop using unsupervised, closed-response online assessments for credit; grade reasoning and process, not just final answers; use paper and whiteboarding, including short oral spot checks; require disclosure and critique of AI use; shift weight to labs, modeling, data analysis, and design; redesign problems around Fermi estimates, novel contexts, and **diagnose-and-repair tasks**; hold major certification in supervised settings and require process evidence on take-home work — [arXiv:2508.08842](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.08842); *The Physics Teacher* (Jan 2026) per [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/when-is-it-time-to-jump-the-boiling-frog-problem-of-ai-use-in-physics-education)
- Babayeva, Widenhorn & Kortemeyer (2026): with answers cheap, courses should assess "ways of knowing" — justifying claims, connecting models to evidence — and use AI as a reasoning prompter and feedback tool under human oversight. AI exam grading is human-in-the-loop: IRT thresholds auto-accept only high-confidence "correct" judgments (R² ≈ 0.91 with TA scores when about half are auto-graded, ≈ 0.96 when about a fifth are); a planned workflow lets students veto AI-proposed grades — [Europhysics News 57(2)](https://www.europhysicsnews.org/10.1051/epn/2026210)
- UK physics degree audit (Pimbblet & Morrell): "business as usual" isn't viable; open-book, take-home, and standard coursework are highly vulnerable; recommend more invigilated exams, vivas (especially on projects), lab skills tests, and presentations; consider testing core disciplinary knowledge directly so students can use AI critically; AI detection is unreliable and can disadvantage non-native English writers — [arXiv:2412.01312](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01312)
- Interviews with 12 physics professors at one Scandinavian research university (2024): the "threat to genuine learning and assessment" frame appeared about twice as often as any other and colored the rest; 8 of 12 had moved to or kept controlled formats (oral exams, supervised tests without aids), one switched to oral exams partly because of GenAI; 8 required declaring AI use; a few assigned AI-use reflection notes; some had students critique AI-generated solutions — Skogvoll & Odden, "How Physics Professors Use and Frame Generative AI Tools," [arXiv:2511.11317](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.11317v1) (preprint; small, single-site)

**Broader higher-ed practice (2026 reporting)**
- AP (April 2026): colleges turning to oral exams after "perfect homework, blank stares." Cornell biomedical engineering (Chris Schaffer): ~70 students submit written problem sets, then 20-minute Socratic oral defenses, with TAs grading only the defenses; a 180-student Cornell engineering course uses four-minute mock interviews; UC San Diego engineering professor Huihui Qi ran a three-year study on scaling oral exams; NYU Stern built an AI-voiced oral exam. - The field is converging on "certify understanding live, grade process," which shifts the burden onto having a process that can be graded quickly and objectively. ## 6. Arguments that explanations checkable against algebra or numbers help detect faked understanding

### Takeaway
I found no study that directly tests the claim that a self-consistent, algebra-mapped toolkit helps students or teachers detect faked understanding. But several adjacent lines of evidence support pieces of it: models (and students using them) produce fluent explanations that are right in words but wrong on the representation; correct answers can come from bad reasoning; students overtrust AI output; physics leaders now recommend grading reasoning and "diagnose-and-repair" tasks; and the successful AI tutor needed human-authored step-by-step solutions to stay accurate. Students who'd worked a problem themselves were the ones who checked units and signs on an AI solution.

### Cited Findings
- Vision-language models "could state plausible physics strategies while misreading the visual representation the task depended on," and strong scores "don't show that the models understand physics in the educational sense used in PER" — [Polverini thesis, Uppsala 2026](https://www.uu.se/en/events/defences/2026-06-09-giulia-polverini-vision-language-model-performance-on-physics-tasks-exploring-ai-capabilities-on-research-based-assessments-involving-visual-representations)
- "False positives": some correct final answers came from flawed reasoning or flawed visual descriptions — [Tufino et al., arXiv:2605.09602](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09602) (preprint)
- Early chatbot reasoning was "yes locally, not globally": GPT-3.5 explanations mixed relevant concepts with irrelevant details and drew inconsistent conclusions, worst on directional reasoning; even GPT-4 overgeneralized (e.g., claiming all forces come in equal-and-opposite pairs) — [West, arXiv:2303.01067](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01067) (preprint)
- ChatGPT in 2023 showed "many of the preconceptions and errors of a beginning learner," and numerical errors hit about half its numerical homework problems — [Kortemeyer, arXiv:2301.12127](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12127)
- Nearly half of ChatGPT-supported physics solutions were wrongly assumed correct by the students who produced them — [Krupp et al., arXiv:2309.03087](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2309.03087) (preprint, small n)
- Pre-service physics teachers (n = 20) reviewing AI solutions to an electric-field problem: none accepted the solution on first reading; their concerns centered on skipped or unexplained steps and whether they could *trace the mathematics*; those who'd solved the problem at least partly checked units and signs explicitly; validation criteria included correctness of mathematical procedures and physical reasonableness (units, sign) — Suárez & González, "Students' Validation Criteria for AI-Generated Physics Solutions," [arXiv:2606.16830](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.16830) (preprint; qualitative, small, all AI solutions happened to be correct)
- Recommendation to grade reasoning and process and to use diagnose-and-repair tasks and oral spot checks — [Kortemeyer, arXiv:2508.08842](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.08842); assess "justifying claims and connecting models to evidence" — [Babayeva et al., EPN 2026](https://www.europhysicsnews.org/10.1051/epn/2026210)
- Test core disciplinary knowledge directly so students can be "maximally intelligent users" who check and question AI output — [Pimbblet & Morrell, arXiv:2412.01312](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01312)
- The Harvard tutor's accuracy relied on pre-written step-by-step solutions, and the platform had to enforce part-by-part sequencing because prompting alone couldn't — [Kestin et al., Sci. Rep. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/); ETH's Ethel feedback tool was anchored to an instructor's sample solution, and AI exam grading auto-accepts only high-confidence judgments, flagging uncertain cases — [Babayeva et al., EPN 2026](https://www.europhysicsnews.org/10.1051/epn/2026210)
- Students using unguarded AI overestimated their own learning — [Knowledge at Wharton on Bastani et al.](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/)
- In an MSU course, unsupervised quiz scores tracked help-seeking patterns while supervised exam scores didn't — i.e., the unsupervised numbers stopped measuring understanding — [Kortemeyer & Bauer, PRPER 2024](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2312.15334)

### Inferences
- The evidence supports a narrower, defensible version of the educator's claim: (a) verbal fluency is no longer evidence of understanding, for students *or* for models; (b) the places where models and AI-reliant students go wrong (directions, diagrams, right-hand rules, multi-step consistency, numerical slips) are places where a step-by-step algebraic or numerical check would expose the error; (c) the checking skill appears to depend on having done the work oneself (Suárez & González; Lehmann et al.'s substitution finding); and (d) every effective AI tutor or AI grader found here was anchored to human-authored, checkable worked solutions. That the *toolkit itself* improves detection is a hypothesis these sources make plausible but don't test.
- A risk worth flagging honestly: models are now good at algebra and arithmetic too (GPT-5 Thinking "reasons symbolically and checks its work"), so checkable algebra on *take-home* work doesn't by itself stop faking; it helps where the check is done live (oral spot checks, in-class whiteboarding) or where the student must reproduce or repair a chain under observation.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found that compares learning or faking-detection under a consistent, algebra-checkable explanatory system vs conventional mixed verbal/symbolic instruction in the AI era.
- No source found on whether checkable numeric explanations help *teachers* detect AI-assisted work more reliably than other formats; the oral-exam reporting is anecdotal on this point.
