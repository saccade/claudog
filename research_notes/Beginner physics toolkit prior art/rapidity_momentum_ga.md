## Rapidity: origin, naming, k-calculus, collider use, textbooks, pedagogy, the rocket

### Takeaway
Rapidity goes back to 1910 (Varićak; Whittaker) and was named by Robb in 1911. It's a routine working variable in collider physics, where differences in rapidity don't change under boosts along the beam, and it appears in graduate and some undergraduate relativity texts (Rindler; Taylor & Wheeler 1st ed.). Bondi's 1964 k-calculus put the Doppler factor first for lay readers (k-factors multiply, so their logs, the rapidities, add). Beginner-level pedagogy for rapidity exists mostly as journal proposals (Lévy-Leblond 1980; Wilson ~2007), and none of it reports classroom outcome data.

### Cited Findings
**Origin and naming**
- Minkowski (1908) treated the Lorentz transformation as a hyperbolic rotation ("rotation through an imaginary angle"). In one space dimension that angle is an additive measure of velocity. — [Wikipedia: Rapidity (pointer)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapidity)
- Vladimir Varićak (1910, *Physikalische Zeitschrift*, "Application of Lobachevskian Geometry in the Theory of Relativity") and E. T. Whittaker (1910, *A History of the Theories of Aether and Electricity*, p. 441) both introduced rapidity as a stand-in for velocity. — [Wikipedia: Rapidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapidity)
- Alfred A. Robb named it "rapidity" in *Optical Geometry of Motion: A New View of the Theory of Relativity* (Cambridge: W. Heffer & Sons, 1911). — [Wikipedia: Rapidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapidity); scan at [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/opticalgeometryo00robbrich)
- Later early uses: Borel (1913, *Comptes rendus* 156–157); Silberstein, *The Theory of Relativity* (Macmillan, 1914, p. 179); Karapetoff, "Restricted relativity in terms of hyperbolic functions of rapidities," *Amer. Math. Monthly* 43, 70 (1936). Historiography: S. Walter, "The non-Euclidean style of Minkowskian relativity," in J. J. Gray (ed.), *The Symbolic Universe* (OUP, 1999), 91–127. — [Wikipedia: Rapidity, reference list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapidity); [Walter PDF (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/20131016142709/http://www.univ-nancy2.fr/DepPhilo/walter/papers/nes.pdf)

**Doppler factor and Bondi's k-calculus**
- E. A. Milne used the method first, in *Relativity, Gravitation and World Structure* (OUP, 1935, pp. 36–38), with a letter for a constant Doppler factor and a treatment of non-inertial motion too. Hermann Bondi renamed it "k-calculus" in *Relativity and Common Sense* (New York: Doubleday, 1964; Heinemann, 1965; Dover reprint, 1980). The book grew out of articles he wrote for *The Illustrated London News* in 1962, and in it Bondi calls k "a fundamental ratio." Rapidity can be defined from k (k = e^rapidity with c = 1), and rapidities add because k-factors multiply. — [Wikipedia: Bondi k-calculus (pointer)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bondi_k-calculus)
- University texts that use the k-calculus: d'Inverno, *Introducing Einstein's Relativity* (Clarendon, 1992), ch. 2; N. M. J. Woodhouse, *Special Relativity* (Springer, 2003); Mason & Woodhouse's Oxford lecture notes. — [Wikipedia: Bondi k-calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bondi_k-calculus)
- A. T. Wilson (BYU Mathematics), "Using ordinary multiplication to do relativistic velocity addition," arXiv:physics/0611192. Wilson proposes the "velocity factor," a two-way Doppler factor equal to k², so that velocity addition becomes multiplication, with rapidity α = ½ ln f. He calls Bondi's k-calculus elegant and accessible but notes it's "not a standard part of introductory physics pedagogy," calls velocity addition a "pedagogical cul-de-sac," and finds it puzzling that rapidities are left out of intro courses now that calculators do hyperbolic functions. — [arXiv:physics/0611192](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0611192)

**Pedagogy papers**
- J.-M. Lévy-Leblond, "Speed(s)," *Am. J. Phys.* 48, 345 (1980). As Wilson summarizes it, rapidity is the integral of proper acceleration over proper time, which is the velocity an ideal Newtonian inertial-guidance system would report. — cited in [Wilson, arXiv:0706.3247](https://arxiv.org/abs/0706.3247)
- A. T. Wilson, "Relativistic rapidity as change in musical pitch," arXiv:0706.3247 (2007, going by the arXiv number). Rapidity is the change in pitch of radiation seen dead ahead versus dead behind, which makes its additivity intuitive. No journal reference is shown on arXiv. — [arXiv:0706.3247](https://arxiv.org/abs/0706.3247)
- Tevian Dray, *The Geometry of Special Relativity* (Taylor & Francis / CRC, 1st ed. 2012; 2nd ed. exists). It treats SR as hyperbolic trigonometry and was developed in Oregon State's NSF-funded Paradigms in Physics project, where it's been used for more than five years. The claim that it was "used successfully" isn't backed by any data. — [Dray book page](https://sites.science.oregonstate.edu/~tevian/geometry); [2nd ed. listing](https://www.lavoisier.fr/livre/mathematiques/the-geometry-of-special-relativity/descriptif_4469986)
- Rhodes & Semon, "Relativistic velocity space, Wigner rotation, and Thomas precession," *Am. J. Phys.* 72(7), 943–960 (2004), doi:10.1119/1.1652040, treats rapidity space in 3D. — [Wikipedia: Rapidity, reference list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapidity) (page range not verified there)

**Textbooks**
- W. Rindler, *Relativity: Special, General, and Cosmological* (OUP, 2001), p. 53, uses rapidity. — [Wikipedia: Rapidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapidity)
- J. D. Jackson, *Classical Electrodynamics*, 3rd ed. (Wiley, 1999), ch. 11, uses rapidity (the boost parameter). — [Wikipedia: Rapidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapidity)
- Taylor & Wheeler, *Spacetime Physics*: according to a PHYS-L post by Bob Sciamanda (Edinboro U., emeritus), the first edition (1966) discusses the "velocity parameter" (that is, rapidity) "at length," but the second edition (1992) "dropped [it] from consideration and never even mentioned" it. The 2nd-ed. table of contents does show no "velocity parameter" entry; it has §2.9 "Rocket Frame," §5.3 "Invariant Hyperbola," and §L.7 "Addition of Velocities." — [PHYS-L post](https://phys-l.org/archives/1999/09_1999/msg00031.html); [2nd-ed. TOC (eftaylor.com)](https://www.eftaylor.com/spacetimephysics/00_cover_toc.pdf)

**Particle physics (routine use today)**
- At hadron colliders, rapidity y is defined from the energy and the momentum component along the beam. Pseudorapidity η = −ln tan(θ/2) depends only on the polar angle and equals y for massless or ultra-relativistic particles. A boost along the beam shifts every y by the same constant, so rapidity differences are boost-invariant; they "transform additively, similar to velocities in Galilean relativity." Rapidity is natural for colliders because particle production is roughly flat in rapidity, and because colliding partons carry different momentum fractions, each collision's rest frame has a different longitudinal boost. — [Wikipedia: Pseudorapidity (pointer)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudorapidity), citing C.-Y. Wong, *Introduction to High-Energy Heavy-Ion Collisions*, p. 24
- The Particle Data Group's kinematics review covers rapidity and pseudorapidity (Amsler et al., *Phys. Lett. B* 667, 1 (2008), §38.5.2). — [PDG 2009 kinematics review](http://pdg.lbl.gov/2009/reviews/rpp2009-rev-kinematics.pdf)

**Relativistic rocket**
- Proper acceleration is the rate of change of rapidity with respect to proper time, so at constant proper acceleration a, rapidity = aτ/c grows linearly in proper time. — [Wikipedia: Rapidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapidity); Lévy-Leblond 1980 as summarized in [Wilson 0706.3247](https://arxiv.org/abs/0706.3247)
- Born (1909, *Ann. Phys.* 335) coined "hyperbolic motion" for constant proper acceleration; Sommerfeld (1910) simplified and extended it. — [Wikipedia: Hyperbolic motion (pointer)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_motion_(relativity))

### Inferences
- Rapidity follows a pattern: it's standard where people compose many boosts or need boost-invariant bookkeeping (colliders, graduate SR), and it gets dropped from mass-market intro texts. The clearest case is Taylor & Wheeler cutting it between editions (single-source, see Gaps).
- The Doppler-factor route (Milne → Bondi → Wilson) is the closest prior art to "rapidity as the log of the Doppler factor" for beginners. Bondi aimed at a lay audience, so beginner-level framing isn't new; what's missing is an evaluation of it.
- Wilson's musical-pitch reading (octaves of Doppler shift) lines up closely with a "log of the Doppler factor" presentation.

### Gaps
- I didn't read Lévy-Leblond's "Speed(s)" itself (AJP 48, 345, 1980, doi probably 10.1119/1.12095, unverified). Its content here comes from Wilson's summary.
- I couldn't confirm what Taylor & Wheeler's 1st edition (1966) says about the "velocity parameter" or why it was cut, beyond one PHYS-L post. The 2nd-ed. TOC is consistent with that post but doesn't prove it.
- I didn't find any Tevian Dray paper on rapidity specifically, only the book, and the book page doesn't use the word "rapidity."
- I found no classroom outcome study (pre/post or comparison) for teaching rapidity or the k-calculus to beginners.
- I didn't check Wilson's journal publication status. The arXiv pages show none.

## Momentum-first mechanics: Newton, Matter & Interactions, outcomes, relativity view

### Takeaway
Newton's "quantity of motion" (Definition II) and his Second Law are both stated as momentum and its change. Chabay & Sherwood's *Matter & Interactions* (NC State) builds intro mechanics on the momentum principle (Δp = F_net Δt, iterated computationally), along with the energy and angular-momentum principles. It's used at Georgia Tech, NC State, Purdue, and elsewhere. The outcome evidence is mixed: in a 5000-student Georgia Tech study, M&I students did worse on the FCI, but across 2000+ students at four universities they did better on the E&M BEMA.

### Cited Findings
- Newton, *Principia* (3rd ed. 1726; Motte transl. 1729), Definition II: "The quantity of motion is the measure of the same, arising from the velocity and the quantity of matter conjunctly." Law II: "The alteration of motion is ever proportional to the motive force impressed; and is made in the direction of the right line in which that force is impressed." — [Physics Hypertextbook, Newton quotes](https://physics.info/newton-second/quote-newton.shtml)
- Matter & Interactions (M&I) was developed by Ruth Chabay and Bruce Sherwood at NC State. Its mechanics rests on three principles (momentum, energy, angular momentum). The momentum principle is a discrete form of Newton's second law and replaces many constant-acceleration kinematics formulas. — [Kohlmyer et al., ASEE 2008, AC 2008-1166](https://peer.asee.org/3715.pdf)
- Chabay's 2004 KSU colloquium describes the goal of explaining a broad range of phenomena "using only a very small number of powerful fundamental principles," and says M&I "emphasizes the power of fundamental principles." — [KSU colloquium abstract](https://www.phys.ksu.edu/news/colloquia/2004/fall/chabay.html)
- Adoption at Georgia Tech: a pilot of about 40 students in Summer 2006, growing to about 30% of the intro sequence by Spring 2008 (300 students each in M&I I and II). NC State and Purdue took part in a joint reform-assessment project. — [ASEE 2008](https://peer.asee.org/3715.pdf)
- Caballero's thesis (Georgia Tech, 2011/12) reports that M&I was about half of Georgia Tech's mechanics enrollment for several years and had been adopted at high schools, two-year colleges, and universities. In it, students apply the momentum principle iteratively over short time steps (computational modeling). — [Caballero thesis, arXiv:1112.5593](https://arxiv.org/abs/1112.5593)
- Mechanics outcome: Caballero, Greco, Murray, Schatz, Bujak, Marr, Catrambone, Kohlmyer, "Comparing large lecture mechanics curricula using the Force Concept Inventory: A five thousand student study," *Am. J. Phys.* 80, 638 (2012) (arXiv:1106.1055). FCI pre-test was 48.9% (traditional) vs. 47.4% (M&I); post-test 71.3% vs. 59.3%, significant after controlling for pre-score, GPA, and SAT. Traditional students outscored M&I in every concept category. The difference tracked how much time each course spent on FCI-type force-and-motion content (homework share 0.57 vs. 0.26; lecture 0.44 vs. 0.26). The authors discuss the limits of concept inventories for judging reform. — [arXiv:1106.1055](https://arxiv.org/abs/1106.1055); [Georgia Tech PDF](https://schatzlab.gatech.edu/Website2013/Library/Caballero2012a.pdf)
- Earlier single-section data (Fall 2006): FCI normalized gain 0.23 (M&I) vs. 0.34–0.48 (traditional). — [ASEE 2008](https://peer.asee.org/3715.pdf)
- E&M outcome: Kohlmyer, Caballero, Catrambone, Chabay, Ding, Haugan, Marr, Sherwood, Schatz, "Tale of two curricula: The performance of 2000 students in introductory electromagnetism," *Phys. Rev. ST Phys. Educ. Res.* 5, 020105 (2009), doi:10.1103/PhysRevSTPER.5.020105. M&I post-test BEMA averages were significantly higher at each of four universities, and the difference wasn't explained by pre-score, GPA, or SAT. The authors credit M&I's reordered progression (vector fields early, microscopic effects of fields on matter). — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/dd6965d104f8434daa4272fd43707a98); [APS](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevSTPER.5.020105)
- On a Spring 2007 E&M final, for a magnetic-induction problem, 28% of M&I vs. 17% of traditional students were fully correct, and 15% vs. 43% used the wrong principle. — [ASEE 2008](https://peer.asee.org/3715.pdf)

### Inferences
- M&I's FCI deficit is best read as a coverage tradeoff (less time on FCI-style force-and-motion items), not as evidence that momentum-first is harder. Caballero et al. frame it that way themselves.
- In relativity, momentum is the more natural primary quantity. The four-momentum (E, p) transforms linearly and is conserved, while velocities don't add linearly. Rapidity bridges the two (p = m sinh(rapidity), E = m cosh(rapidity), c = 1). This is standard textbook physics, but I didn't fetch a specific source stating the "momentum, not velocity, is fundamental" view (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- I didn't fetch the publisher page for M&I's current edition (Wiley, 4th ed. ~2015; a 5th ed. may exist), and found no current count of adopting institutions.
- I found no source quoting a relativity textbook saying outright that four-momentum rather than velocity is fundamental. The claim is sound physics but has no citation here.
- I didn't verify Purdue's current use of M&I.
- I didn't search for other momentum-first intro curricula (e.g., Karlsruhe Physics Course "Impuls" as a substance-like quantity; Moore's *Six Ideas That Shaped Physics*, Unit C, conservation-first). These are worth a follow-up.

## Geometric algebra replacing cross products and hand rules

### Takeaway
Hestenes revived Clifford's geometric algebra (GA) for physics. His *New Foundations for Classical Mechanics* (1986; 2nd ed. 1999) is a mechanics text built on it, and his 2002 Oersted Medal Lecture (AJP 2003) argued for GA as the unified language of physics, with the axial vector shown to be "just a bivector represented by its dual." GA is used routinely in parts of mathematical physics, computer graphics, and robotics (Doran & Lasenby's Cambridge group), but it hasn't entered standard intro physics. Hestenes himself never taught GA to undergraduates. Recent proposals (Jensen & Poling 2023; Jensen 2024; Chappell et al.; Horn 2013) argue for bivectors and tiles at the intro level but report only anecdotal classroom experience.

### Cited Findings
- David Hestenes, *New Foundations for Classical Mechanics*, 2nd ed. (Dordrecht: Kluwer/Springer, 1999), Fundamental Theories of Physics vol. 99, xiv + 706 pp. It's an intermediate mechanics text that introduces GA as a unified language combining vector, complex, and matrix algebra, with extended rotational dynamics and celestial mechanics. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/0-306-47122-1)
- D. Hestenes, "Oersted Medal Lecture 2002: Reforming the mathematical language of physics," *Am. J. Phys.* 71(2), 104–121 (2003); companion paper "Spacetime physics with geometric algebra," *Am. J. Phys.* 71(6), 691– (2003). — [Hestenes site](https://davidhestenes.net/geocalc/html/Oersted-ReformingTheLanguage.html); [PDF](https://davidhestenes.net/geocalc/pdf/OerstedMedalLecture.pdf)
- From the Oersted lecture: the cross product is "defined only in 3D," while the outer product a∧b is intrinsic to the plane of a and b. "The conventional vector cross product a × b is implicitly defined as the dual of the outer product" (a∧b = i a×b). "GA reveals that an axial vector is just a bivector represented by its dual," so the polar/axial distinction is unnecessary. Handedness comes from the ordered product σ1σ2σ3 = i. — [Oersted lecture PDF](https://davidhestenes.net/geocalc/pdf/OerstedMedalLecture.pdf)
- Why it didn't spread, in Hestenes's own account (same lecture, §VIII): "I have not ventured to teach GA in undergraduate courses" (graduate courses only). Demand for GA textbooks is "near zero," which pushed NFCM into a monograph series. Colleagues' reactions were "invariably dismissive as soon as they detected deviation from standard practice." Since GA wasn't used in other courses, students had to learn it alone. A colleague who adopted NFCM was denied tenure. He cites Dyson that new mathematical ideas take "fifty to a hundred years" to be generally recognized. — [Oersted lecture PDF](https://davidhestenes.net/geocalc/pdf/OerstedMedalLecture.pdf)
- C. Doran & A. Lasenby, *Geometric Algebra for Physicists* (Cambridge UP, 2003; ISBN 0-521-48022-1, 578 pp.). It presents GA "as a new mathematical tool to add to your existing set" and introduces it through applications, building on Clifford (1870s), Hamilton, and Grassmann. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/geometric-algebra-for-physicists/preface/8C12156C5B2AD9400D6E0A5B07A0929F); [Math. Gazette review record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/mathematical-gazette/article/abs/geometric-algebra-for-physicists-by-chris-doran-and-anthony-lasenby-pp-578-6500-2003-isbn-0-521-48022-1-cambridge-university-press/4D142B302E716C459ADBACE7E4DE0C5E)
- Chappell, Iqbal & Abbott (U. Adelaide), "Geometric Algebra: A natural representation of three-space," arXiv:1101.3619. They argue GA subsumes Euclid, Cartesian coordinates, complex numbers, quaternions, and Gibbs vectors, conclude it "appears to be an excellent formalism to introduce into high-school and undergraduate university curricula," and note that dot and cross products needn't be defined separately. No classroom data. Related: "A simplified approach to electromagnetism using geometric algebra," arXiv:1010.4947. — [arXiv:1101.3619](https://arxiv.org/abs/1101.3619); [arXiv:1010.4947](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1010.4947)
- S. Jensen & J. Poling (Alma College), "Teaching Rotational Physics with Bivectors," arXiv:2207.03560 (manuscript dated Aug 2023, prepared for AJP). Angular velocity and angular momentum are taught as oriented "tiles," avoiding right-hand rules. Preliminary experience suggests tiles reduce students' tendency to treat angular quantities as linear velocities or forces. The wedge product as the first step of the right-hand rule was "most effective," but teaching the bivector and vector languages side by side "seemed repetitive and confusing." The paper cites prior PER showing poor cross-product performance and that many students think angular velocity lies in the plane of motion. — [arXiv:2207.03560](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03560)
- S. Jensen, "Teaching Magnetism with Bivectors," arXiv:2309.02548 (v2 Nov 2024). B is a bivector (oriented tile) rather than a pseudovector. The paper reports no systematic outcome data, only informal "in the author's experience" remarks. — [arXiv:2309.02548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02548)
- M. E. Horn, "Didaktische Konzeption eines Lehrbuches zur Geometrischen Algebra," DPG Frühjahrstagung Didaktik der Physik, Jena 2013 (PhyDid B). Describes a university-level GA textbook with algebraic (Pauli-matrix) and geometric entry paths, and notes limited uptake in German physics didactics. No empirical data. — [PhyDid B PDF](https://ojs.dpg-physik.de/index.php/phydid-b/article/download/475/615)

### Inferences
- The barriers Hestenes names are institutional (textbook demand, lack of use in later courses, career risk, conservative curricula), not evidence that students can't learn GA. Jensen & Poling's "side by side is confusing" remark suggests a partial transition (bivectors plus the usual cross product) may cost more than either alone.
- Recent intro-level GA work narrows the goal to bivectors and "tiles" for rotation and magnetism rather than full GA, which is closest to a toolkit that drops hand rules without teaching the whole algebra.

### Gaps
- I didn't fetch a source for NFCM's 1st edition (D. Reidel, 1986).
- I didn't fetch Alan Macdonald, *Linear and Geometric Algebra* (self-published via CreateSpace, ~2010) or its companion *Vector and Geometric Calculus* (~2012). Details are from memory and unverified.
- I found no controlled study (pre/post, comparison group) of GA or bivectors with high school or intro undergraduate students. Published claims are proposals or anecdote.
- I didn't search for GA use in computer graphics and robotics (e.g., Dorst, Fontijne & Mann, *Geometric Algebra for Computer Science*, 2007; the AGACSE conference series), which is where GA is most routinely used outside physics.

## Hestenes's other line: Modeling Instruction and the Force Concept Inventory (a reform that spread)

### Takeaway
Hestenes's PER work spread widely. The FCI (Hestenes, Wells & Swackhamer, 1992) became the standard mechanics concept test and the basis of Hake's 6000-student comparison. Modeling Instruction (Hestenes with high school teacher Malcolm Wells; NSF-funded 1990–2005) had reached more than 10,200 teachers in 49 states by 2018 (at least 10% of U.S. physics teachers) and is now carried on by the teacher-founded AMTA (2005).

### Cited Findings
- D. Hestenes, M. Wells & G. Swackhamer, "Force Concept Inventory," *Phys. Teach.* 30(3), 141 (1992). About half its items come from the Mechanics Diagnostic Test: I. Halloun & D. Hestenes, "The initial knowledge state of college physics students," *Am. J. Phys.* 53(11), 1043 (1985). More than 50 published FCI studies cover 70+ institutions and 35,000+ students. Hake (1998) compared about 6,500 students across methods. A larger meta-analysis (about 31,000 students in 450 classes) gives average normalized gain 0.39 for interactive engagement vs. 0.22 for traditional lecture. — [PhysPort: FCI](https://physport.org/assessments/FCI)
- R. R. Hake, "Interactive-engagement versus traditional methods: A six-thousand-student survey of mechanics test data for introductory physics courses," *Am. J. Phys.* 66, 64 (1998). — [Hake 1998 PDF](https://web.mit.edu/jrankin/www/Active_Learning/hake_active_phys.pdf)
- Modeling Instruction was developed by David Hestenes (ASU) and Malcolm Wells (Tempe high school physics teacher), NSF-funded 1990–2005, and institutionalized at ASU in 2001. As of 2018, more than 10,200 teachers in 49 states had taken Modeling Workshops, including at least 10% of U.S. physics teachers. In a typical summer, AMTA ran about 60 multi-week workshops at 30 sites in 20 states for about 1,000 teachers. On FCI data from 20,000 high school students, Modeling gains were typically about double those of traditional instruction. U.S. Department of Education expert panels rated it an "Exemplary" K-12 science program and a "Promising" educational technology program. APS Excellence in Physics Education Award 2014. — [Jackson & Hestenes, APS Forum on Education newsletter, Spring 2019](https://engage.aps.org/fed/resources/newsletters/spring-2019)
- AMTA was founded in 2005 by STEM teachers, partly because NSF funding was ending. Workshops had been held at 60 institutions. ASU's summer MNS program draws 125–150 teachers a year. — [AMTA history, "Stage 5 2005 to present"](https://www.modelinginstruction.org/?p=774)
- AMTA's 2024 annual report shows a smaller current scale: 30 workshops with 194 participants (77 in physics mechanics), plus distance courses (51 participants; the report also gives 69 elsewhere), total income about $295k, expenses about $326k, and a net operating loss of about $32k. — [AMTA Annual Report 2024](https://www.modelinginstruction.org/annual-report-2024/)

### Inferences
- The contrast is the useful lesson. The reform that spread (Modeling, FCI) changed classroom practice and gave teachers a shared measuring tool, and it kept standard notation. The reform that didn't spread (GA) asked everyone downstream to change notation. Teacher-run workshops plus a cheap, common assessment carried Modeling; GA had neither.
- AMTA's 2024 numbers (about 200 in-person workshop participants) are far below the "about 1,000 per summer" in the 2019 article, which suggests participation has declined, possibly after COVID. The two sources may count differently, so treat this as indicative.

- I found no current cumulative count of teachers trained (after 2018), and no AMTA membership count.
- I didn't verify the year of the U.S. Department of Education designation (often given as 2001).
- I didn't fetch Hestenes's own papers on Modeling theory (e.g., "Toward a modeling theory of physics instruction," *Am. J. Phys.* 55, 440 (1987); Wells, Hestenes & Swackhamer, *Am. J. Phys.* 63, 606 (1995)). Citations are from memory and unverified.
