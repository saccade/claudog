*Claude wrote these research notes (October 25, 2026), from web sources fetched that day. Anything not confirmed against a fetched source is marked "unverified" or listed under Gaps.*

## 1. Force dynamics and embodied semantics (Talmy; Lakoff and Johnson; image schemas) and their relation to naive physics

### Takeaway
Talmy's force dynamics shows that English grammar (verbs like *keep*, *let*, *despite*, *make*, *prevent*) encodes a model in which a focal object (the Agonist) has an intrinsic tendency toward motion or rest that an opposing Antagonist does or doesn't overcome. Talmy himself says this model parallels naive physics and medieval impetus theory and is "at considerable variance with modern physics." Image-schema work (Johnson, Lakoff) has been applied to physics teaching mainly through an "effort–resistance–flow" schema, which the authors argue sits at the heart of physics and matches diSessa's "Ohm's p-prim."

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Talmy, L. (1988). "Force dynamics in language and cognition." *Cognitive Science* 12(1), 49–100. Revised as chapter 7 of *Toward a Cognitive Semantics*, Vol. 1 (MIT Press, 2000). — [Wikipedia: Force dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_dynamics); [Talmy chapter 7 PDF, author's site](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~talmy/talmyweb/Volume1/chap7.pdf)
- Core machinery: the force entity in focus is the Agonist, the one opposing it the Antagonist. "Force entities have an intrinsic force tendency, either toward action or toward rest." One entity is stronger, and the resultant (action or rest) depends on both the intrinsic tendency and the balance of strengths. — [Wikipedia: Force dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_dynamics)
- Talmy's diagnostic forms: "keep -ing" is "perhaps the single form most indicative of the presence of force dynamics" (example: "The ball kept rolling because of the wind blowing on it"). Other patterns: *despite* ("The shed kept standing despite the gale wind blowing against it") and *let* ("The plug's coming loose let the water flow from the tank"). — [Talmy ch. 7, §2.1–2.2, pp. 414–420](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~talmy/talmyweb/Volume1/chap7.pdf)
- Talmy draws the naive-physics link himself. In the introduction (p. 410), the linguistic system "shows close parallels with the conceptual systems for force interaction both in naive physics and psychology, and in early science." In §9 ("Conceptual Models of Physics and Psychology," pp. 456–457) he says the intrinsic-tendency concept "appears to correlate with historically earlier scientific theories involving an object's impetus in motion," that it "is at considerable variance with modern physics," that "Objects have no internal impulsion toward some state of activity," and that in physics "stationariness is not a distinct state set apart from motion." He also notes there's "no physical principle for differentiating equivalent objects according to 'privilege'" (that is, the Agonist/Antagonist asymmetry has no counterpart in Newton's third law). He cites diSessa's intuitive-physics work as showing "striking parallels." — [Talmy ch. 7](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~talmy/talmyweb/Volume1/chap7.pdf)
- Deane (1996), as quoted in the Wikipedia article, grounds force-dynamic concepts in motor activity: "Such concepts have an obvious base in ordinary motor activities"; "the brain must be able to calculate the force vector produced by muscular exertion." — [Wikipedia: Force dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_dynamics)
- Image-schema references as cited in physics-education work: Johnson, M. (1987), *The Body in the Mind* (U. Chicago Press); Lakoff & Johnson (1980), *Metaphors We Live By* (U. Chicago Press); Lakoff (1987), *Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things*; Lakoff (1993), "The contemporary theory of metaphor." Image schemas are described as "pre-linguistic structures acquired through our basic bodily experiences" (part–whole, source–path–target, up–down, center–periphery, container, balance, blocking). — [Bruun & Christiansen, "Kinesthetic activities in physics instruction: Image schematic justification and design based on didactic situations," arXiv:1410.4602](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1410.4602)
- Bruun & Christiansen argue that "The effort-resistance-flow schema is not at odds with physics but lies rather at the heart of physics"; that Johnson's force-based schemas (e.g., blocking = effort against resistance with no flow) are special cases of it; and that it is "likely central to the sense of mechanism (diSessa 1993)." They tie it to diSessa's Ohm's p-prim: "an agent that is the locus of an impetus that acts against a resistance to produce some sort of result" (diSessa 1993, p. 126). They use kinesthetic activities (students enacting effort, resistance, and flow) and then map them onto formal quantities. — [arXiv:1410.4602](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1410.4602)
- Robertson, Goodhew, Scherr & Heron (2021), "Impetus-Like Reasoning as Continuous with Newtonian Physics," *The Physics Teacher* 59(3), 185–188, doi:10.1119/10.0003660, argue (with data, in a "resources" framework) that impetus-like ideas connect to Newtonian physics rather than being simply wrong; they re-analyze Clement's tossed-coin task and McCloskey et al.'s curved-tube task. — [PER Central record](https://www.per-central.org/items/detail.cfm?ID=16464)

### Inferences
- For a vocabulary that ties verbs to quantities, Talmy's analysis is a direct warning label: the commonest English force verbs (*keep*, *let*, *hold back*, *overcome*, *despite*) come with an Agonist that "wants" to move or rest. That's an impetus picture baked into grammar. Verbs whose dictionary sense already names an interaction between two bodies, or a change in motion, fit the physics better than verbs that name a tendency inside one body.
- Talmy's "privilege" point (one entity is the focus, the other is background) is the linguistic shadow of the well-documented third-law trouble (FCI's "most active agent produces the greatest force," §2 below). A word list where each push verb has a natural pair, or names the interaction rather than the doer, would address it at the level of grammar.
- The effort–resistance–flow schema (Ohm's p-prim) is a case where an intuition is already right for many physics settings (current/voltage/resistance, drag, flow). It's an example of a "productive" intuition to build on rather than replace, consistent with the resources view (Robertson et al. 2021) and Clement's anchoring examples (§2).

### Gaps
- I did not fetch the original *Cognitive Science* 1988 article; quotations and page numbers are from Talmy's revised 2000 chapter on his own site. Wording may differ slightly from the 1988 paper.
- I didn't find a study that tests whether choosing force-dynamically "neutral" or Newton-compatible verbs improves learning. The idea is supported by argument (Talmy; Brookes & Etkina, §3), not by a controlled trial I could locate.
- Venue and year of Bruun & Christiansen weren't stated in the fetched arXiv text (only the arXiv number, 1410.4602, which implies an October 2014 posting).
- Lakoff and Johnson's own texts weren't fetched; their details above are as cited by Bruun & Christiansen.

## 2. Naive physics and misconceptions (McCloskey; Halloun & Hestenes; diSessa; Clement; FCI; Hake)

### Takeaway
Beginners arrive with a coherent-looking but non-Newtonian "common sense" mechanics (impetus, motion-implies-force, the stronger or more active agent pushes harder), and conventional lecture courses barely move it: about 14% gain on the 1985 Mechanics Diagnostic, a normalized gain ⟨g⟩ of 0.23 ± 0.04 for traditional courses versus 0.48 ± 0.14 for interactive-engagement courses in Hake's 6,542-student survey. diSessa reframed these beliefs as many small, context-cued "phenomenological primitives" rather than a single theory, and that "knowledge in pieces" view underlies later work that treats intuitions as resources to refine.

### Cited Findings
**McCloskey (impetus).**
- Citation: McCloskey, M. (1983). "Intuitive physics." *Scientific American* 248(4), 122–130, doi:10.1038/scientificamerican0483-122. — [Academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/28201242/Intuitive_Physics)
- Findings reported there: only 45% of students knew a ball dropped by a walking person travels forward as it falls (49% said straight down, 6% backward); on a ball whirled on a string, 51% drew the correct straight tangent after release and 30% drew a curve; on a ball rolling off a cliff, 28% drew the correct parabola and more than a third drew "straight out, then down" (Albert of Saxony's medieval answer). For the belief that motion is maintained by a "force of motion," 93% held it before instruction and 80% still did after the course. McCloskey explicitly traces this to medieval impetus theory (Philoponus, Buridan, Albert of Saxony). — [McCloskey 1983](https://www.academia.edu/28201242/Intuitive_Physics)
- The curved-tube ("curvilinear impetus") study is McCloskey, Caramazza & Green (1980, *Science*); it's cited by Robertson et al. 2021. — [PER Central record for Robertson et al.](https://www.per-central.org/items/detail.cfm?ID=16464) (original *Science* paper not fetched)

**Halloun & Hestenes (1985).**
- Citation: Halloun, I. A., & Hestenes, D. (1985). "The initial knowledge state of college physics students." *Am. J. Phys.* 53(11), 1043–1048. — [Hestenes's site PDF](https://davidhestenes.net/modeling/R&E/InitialKnowledge.pdf)
- About 1,500 Arizona State University students and 80 high-school students. Students' "common-sense beliefs" about motion "strongly affect" performance; conventional instruction changes them only slightly: "The small values (14%) for the gain indicate that conventional instruction has little effect on the student's basic knowledge state." The gain was essentially the same across four classes taught by different professors with the same textbook. Even A students averaged only about 75% on the post-test. — [Halloun & Hestenes 1985](https://davidhestenes.net/modeling/R&E/InitialKnowledge.pdf)
- Math and mechanics pretest scores were only weakly correlated (0.32, falling to 0.19 when certain reasoning items were removed): "high mathematical competence is not sufficient for high performance in physics." The two pretests together explained about 42% of course-performance variance, versus about 15% for background courses. — [Halloun & Hestenes 1985](https://davidhestenes.net/modeling/R&E/InitialKnowledge.pdf)

**Force Concept Inventory (1992).**
- Citation: Hestenes, D., Wells, M., & Swackhamer, G. (1992). "Force Concept Inventory." *The Physics Teacher* 30, 141–151 (March 1992). — [PDF via gwern.net](https://gwern.net/doc/science/physics/1992-hestenes.pdf); [Hestenes's site](https://davidhestenes.net/modeling/R&E/FCI.PDF)
- It's "not a test of intelligence; it is a probe of belief systems." It breaks the Newtonian force concept into six dimensions (kinematics, first law, second law, third law, superposition, kinds of force) and lists 28 commonsense misconceptions in six families: kinematics, impetus (including the "container metaphor," where objects store "go power"), active force (e.g., AF2 "motion implies active force," AF4 "velocity proportional to applied force"), action/reaction (greater mass or the most active agent exerts the greater force), concatenation of influences (the largest or last force determines motion), and other influences (centrifugal force, obstacles exert no force, "heavier objects fall faster"). — [FCI 1992](https://gwern.net/doc/science/physics/1992-hestenes.pdf)
- On language, directly relevant here: students' terms "like 'force,' 'energy,' and 'power' are often used interchangeably"; students "never use the word 'impetus'" but "'force' is perhaps the most common" word they use for it; commonsense beliefs are "metaphorical and vague with situation-dependent meanings." — [FCI 1992](https://gwern.net/doc/science/physics/1992-hestenes.pdf)
- Example pre→post results: Arizona regular high school 27→48%; Arizona AP 41→57% (described as "a mere 5%" average gain on some measure); Harvard regular post-test 77%. The authors propose "a kind of conceptual threshold near 60%," with about 80% marking a Newtonian thinker, and say the score is an upper bound on Newtonian understanding. Conventional instruction's poor result is "independent of the instructor and the mode of instruction." — [FCI 1992](https://gwern.net/doc/science/physics/1992-hestenes.pdf)

**Hake's survey.** (Note: the assignment said 2003; the survey is 1998. Hake wrote several later follow-ups, but the six-thousand-student paper is 1998.)
- Citation: Hake, R. R. (1998). "Interactive-engagement versus traditional methods: A six-thousand-student survey of mechanics test data for introductory physics courses." *Am. J. Phys.* 66(1), 64–74. — [MIT-hosted PDF](https://web.mit.edu/jrankin/www/Active_Learning/hake_active_phys.pdf)
- 62 courses, 6,542 students: 14 traditional courses (N = 2,084), 48 interactive-engagement (IE) courses (N = 4,458). Normalized gain ⟨g⟩ = (post% − pre%)/(100 − pre%). Traditional ⟨g⟩ = 0.23 ± 0.04; IE ⟨g⟩ = 0.48 ± 0.14, a difference of about 1.8 standard deviations of the IE distribution. A subset of 30 courses (N = 3,259) took the Mechanics Baseline problem-solving test, and the results suggest IE also helps problem solving. — [Hake 1998](https://web.mit.edu/jrankin/www/Active_Learning/hake_active_phys.pdf)

**diSessa (p-prims, knowledge in pieces).**
- Citations: diSessa, A. A. (1993). "Toward an epistemology of physics." *Cognition and Instruction* 10(2–3), 105–225, doi:10.1207/s1532690xci1002&3_2 — [Wikipedia: Andrea diSessa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_diSessa). diSessa, A. A. (1988). "Knowledge in pieces." In G. Forman & P. Pufall (Eds.), *Constructivism in the Computer Age*, 49–70 (Erlbaum). — [same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_diSessa). Conflict: Sherin's 2007 PERC slides give the 1993 pages as 165–255 — [Sherin PERC 2007 slides](https://web.phys.ksu.edu/perc2007/formats/sherin-invited.pdf); the DOI-backed 105–225 is more likely correct.
- P-prims make up the "sense of mechanism," "a sub-system of commonsense physics knowledge." Example, Ohm's p-prim (AGENT works against RESISTANCE to produce RESULT): "Things have to work harder in the presence of increased resistance if they want to produce the same result." Other named p-prims include "force as mover" and "dying away" ("unnatural motions die away"). — [Sherin PERC 2007 slides](https://web.phys.ksu.edu/perc2007/formats/sherin-invited.pdf)
- Related: Smith, diSessa & Roschelle (1994), "Misconceptions reconceived," is cited as a key statement of the constructivist, knowledge-in-pieces critique of "misconceptions." — [Wikipedia: Andrea diSessa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_diSessa)

**Clement.**
- Clement (1982), "Students' preconceptions in introductory mechanics," *Am. J. Phys.* 50, 66–71, is the classic tossed-coin study (students draw an upward force on a coin that's rising after release). It's cited in later work on mass, momentum, and force misconceptions. — [EPFL literature review](https://www.epfl.ch/education/teaching/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Students-errors-in-learning-classical-mechanics.pdf); [Robertson et al. 2021 record](https://www.per-central.org/items/detail.cfm?ID=16464)
- Camp & Clement et al., *Preconceptions in Mechanics* (AAPT, 2nd ed. 2010): nine high-school units built around "target problems" that expose a persistent preconception, and "anchoring analogies or examples" where students' intuition already agrees with the physicist's, so that intuition can grow into a rival that eventually "predominate[s] over" the misconception. — [UMass SRRI page](https://srri.umass.edu/preconceptions_in_mechanics)

### Inferences
- The FCI authors' own note that students use "force" for what's really impetus (and use force, energy, and power interchangeably) is the strongest link between the misconception literature and the vocabulary problem: a large part of what the FCI detects is students holding a different referent for the word "force."
- Halloun & Hestenes's weak math–mechanics correlation supports building the conceptual layer in plain language before (or alongside) the algebra. It also cuts the other way: words without the right referents don't help, so a word-to-quantity toolkit has to fix the referent, not just supply the word.
- Hake's ⟨g⟩ gives a concrete yardstick: a new curriculum that gets around 0.5 on the FCI is doing about as well as the IE courses of the 1990s. One that gets around 0.2 is doing what lectures do.
- diSessa's and Clement's "anchor" work suggest the toolkit's operator vocabulary (crane, machine, pilot) may act as anchoring intuitions: practitioner words that already track the right quantity are exactly what Clement's method looks for.

### Gaps
- Clement's 1982 percentages (the oft-quoted share of engineering students drawing an upward force on the tossed coin before and after a course) were not confirmed from a fetched source. I recall roughly 88% before and about 70% after, but this is **unverified** and shouldn't be quoted without checking the AJP paper.
- diSessa's 1983 chapter ("Phenomenology and the evolution of intuition," in Gentner & Stevens, eds., *Mental Models*) is commonly cited, but I didn't confirm its details.
- The McCloskey, Caramazza & Green (1980) *Science* citation details weren't fetched.

## 3. Language in physics teaching (the vocabulary problem; Arons; words versus equations)

### Takeaway
Physics-education research treats everyday meanings of *force*, *momentum*, *impulse*, *heat*, and *energy* as a real barrier. The everyday meaning doesn't get replaced by the physics one; the two coexist. Brookes and Etkina showed the grammar physicists use ("heat flows," "energy in the system") carries ontological commitments that students take literally. Arons's prescription is "idea first and name afterwards." Sherin's "symbolic forms" give the best-developed account of how plain-language ideas map onto the shape of an equation (parts-of-a-whole, base + change, balancing, proportionality), which is close to a research foundation for "every sentence maps onto algebra."

### Cited Findings
**Vocabulary studies.**
- Itza-Ortiz, S. F., Rebello, N. S., & Zollman, D. (with Rodriguez-Achach, M.) (2003). "The vocabulary of introductory physics and its implications for learning physics." *The Physics Teacher* 41 (September 2003). The fetched copy reads "pp. 41–46"; the printed page range (I recall 330–336) is **unverified**. — [KSU PDF](https://perg.phys.ksu.edu/papers/2003/itza-ortiztpt.pdf); [arXiv:physics/0207066](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0207066)
- Study of 154 non-science majors (Hewitt's *Conceptual Physics*) on "force," "momentum," and "impulse," plus Mexican engineering freshmen on "fuerza." 59% of students' pre-course sentences used "force" as a verb; 80% used momentum and impulse as nouns. After the course, only 36% could explain the physics meaning of force. In interviews (14 students), only 2 linked force to mass or acceleration, 6 linked momentum to mass or velocity, and 1 explained impulse. "Momentum" was easier because students "always related it to motion"; "impulse" was harder because its everyday meaning is further from the physics. Spanish speakers read "fuerza" as "poder" (power); the authors suggest German, which separates noun and verb forms, might help. — [arXiv:physics/0207066](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0207066)
- Their conclusion: the physics meaning won't "take the place of the everyday meaning, but rather they would coexist"; they recommend writing assignments where students compare the two meanings. — [arXiv:physics/0207066](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0207066); [KSU PDF](https://perg.phys.ksu.edu/papers/2003/itza-ortiztpt.pdf)
- Williams, H. T. (1999). "Semantics in teaching introductory physics." *Am. J. Phys.* 67, 670 — cited by Itza-Ortiz et al. as research on textbook semantics. — [arXiv:physics/0207066](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0207066) (content not fetched)

**Grammar and metaphor (Brookes & Etkina).**
- Brookes, D. T., & Etkina, E. (2007). "Using conceptual metaphor and functional grammar to explore how language used in physics affects student learning." *Phys. Rev. ST Phys. Educ. Res.* 3, 010105. Physicists' language encodes analogical models through grammar and metaphor; grammar steers students to sort concepts into ontological categories (matter, process, state). "Heat flows from A to B" makes heat a moving thing, though physicists classify heat as a transfer; "energy flows into the system" makes energy a substance in a container. In quantum mechanics, the "particle in a potential well" wording leads to an "exhaustion" misconception (the particle loses energy tunneling). Recommendation: treat language as a representation that needs practice like graphs and equations, and ask students "what do you mean?" when a metaphor is used. — [arXiv:0704.1319](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0704.1319); [APS DOI link](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevSTPER.3.010105)
- Brookes, D. T., & Etkina, E. (2009). "'Force,' ontology, and language." *Phys. Rev. ST Phys. Educ. Res.* 5, 010110, doi:10.1103/PhysRevSTPER.5.010110. Student difficulties "can arise from their use and interpretation of language as well as their physical experiences"; students "redefine and categorize the meaning of formal scientific concepts," "a process remarkably similar to that engaged in by physicists since the time of the ancient Greeks." The *Physics* synopsis concludes that teachers should weigh their word choices. — [APS Physics synopsis, "A force by any other name…"](https://physics.aps.org/articles/v2/s62)

**Arons.**
- Arons, A. B. (1997). *Teaching Introductory Physics.* Wiley, New York, 775 pp. Part I reprints his 1990 *A Guide to Introductory Physics Teaching*; Part II is 400+ conceptual homework and test questions; Part III is a new monograph on energy. Redish's review calls it "probably the best introduction to physics teaching available." — [Redish review, ICPE newsletter](https://physics.umd.edu/icpe/newsletters/n34/Book.htm)
- Arons's language principles, as summarized by Kurki-Suonio (*Eur. J. Phys.* 19, 316–318, 1998): concepts introduced by "idea first and name afterwards"; "Concepts are synthesized out of observational experience"; students should get "direct experience about how words acquire meaning through shared experience"; rote teaching leaves students unable to separate "jargon and knowledge"; Socratic questioning leads to "articulation of operational statements." Arons also stresses "the development of the capacity for arithmetic reasoning," criticizing students who are "not reasoning either arithmetically or algebraically but are simply arranging the symbols." — [Kurki-Suonio review PDF](https://www.mv.helsinki.fi/home/kurkisuo/6.3.C/98-EJP-ARONS.pdf)

**Words versus equations (symbolic forms).**
- Sherin, B. L. (2001). "How students understand physics equations." *Cognition and Instruction* 19(4), 479–541. A symbolic form is "an 'idea' plus a specification of how to express that idea in an equation": a conceptual schema paired with a symbol template. 21 forms in 6 clusters, e.g., parts-of-a-whole (□ + □ + □), base ± change (□ ± □), balancing (□ = □), and prop+ (a quantity in the numerator). — [Sherin PERC 2007 slides](https://web.phys.ksu.edu/perc2007/formats/sherin-invited.pdf)
- Kuo, E., Hull, M. M., Gupta, A., & Elby, A. (2013). "How students blend conceptual and formal mathematical reasoning in solving physics problems." *Science Education* 97(1), 32–57. Examples: v = v₀ + at read as base + change; E = ½mv² + mgh + ½kx² as parts-of-a-whole; terminal speed v = mg/k as prop+ ("heavier objects reach greater terminal velocity"). A student who read the velocity equation as expressing "an overarching conceptual meaning" found a shortcut; a student who treated it as "a tool for numerical computation" didn't. The authors argue "such blended processing indicates a deeper, more expert understanding than simply using the formalism." Small sample (3 of 10 other coded students blended). — [arXiv:1102.3453](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1102.3453)

### Inferences
- The literature diagnoses the vocabulary problem thoroughly but mostly prescribes awareness (compare meanings, ask "what do you mean?"), not word choice. I found no reform curriculum that systematically picked words whose everyday meaning already matches the physics. Itza-Ortiz et al.'s momentum-versus-impulse contrast is the closest empirical hint that a word whose everyday sense already tracks the physics ("momentum" relates to motion) is easier than one that doesn't ("impulse").
- Itza-Ortiz et al.'s "coexist, not replace" result argues for picking words where the everyday and physics senses agree, since the everyday sense won't go away.
- Sherin's symbolic forms are a near-exact research counterpart of "every sentence maps onto algebra": a plain-English construction ("starts at… and changes by…," "made up of…," "balances") corresponds to an equation shape. A toolkit that names its sentence frames to match the forms would have a literature to cite.
- Brookes & Etkina's grammar analysis suggests that choosing whether a quantity is a noun (a thing that can be "in" or "of" something) or a verb (a process) is itself an ontological choice students will take literally. That bears directly on making quantities into verbs or adjectives.

### Gaps
- Williams (1999) wasn't fetched; I can't say what it recommends.
- I didn't find empirical studies specifically of "work" and "power" everyday-versus-physics meanings, though FCI 1992 notes force, energy, and power used interchangeably.
- Rincke (2011, *Int. J. Sci. Educ.*, on learning "force" as like learning a language) turned up in search, but the repository blocked access, so its findings aren't included.
- No study I found tests adopting practitioner (operator, pilot, trades) vocabulary in physics teaching.

## 4. Estimation-first approaches (Fermi problems; order-of-magnitude physics; Mahajan)

### Takeaway
There's a well-developed teaching tradition (Caltech's Ph 101, started by Goldreich and Phinney; Mahajan's thesis and books; Redish's Physics Teacher-style essays) that treats rounding, dimensional analysis, and lumping as reasoning tools, with the explicit aim of "insight over accuracy." Its grading rewards justified reasoning over precision. Empirical evidence on learning outcomes is thin: the sources are mostly practitioner accounts.

### Cited Findings
- Mahajan, S. (1998). *Order of Magnitude Physics: A Textbook with Applications to the Retinal Rod and the Density of Prime Numbers.* PhD thesis, Caltech. Its first five chapters (simple estimates, dimensional analysis, mechanical and thermal properties of materials, water waves) are based on the "Order of Magnitude Physics class taught at Caltech by Peter Goldreich and Sterl Phinney"; Phinney was his advisor. — [Caltech thesis record](https://thesis.library.caltech.edu/5338)
- Caltech Ph 101 (Spring 2025, E. S. Phinney): "Emphasis will be on using basic physics to understand complicated systems." Goals: "unafraid to estimate numbers you don't know, scale solutions, make approximations." "A well-justified roughly correct answer will get full credit"; a reasonably justified but wildly wrong answer gets half credit. Estimates must be worked out before any online search. Recommended reading includes both Mahajan books. — [Ph 101 description](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~oom/description2025.html); [Ph 101 syllabus](https://www.its.caltech.edu/%7Eoom/syllabus2025.html)
- Mahajan, S. (2010). *Street-Fighting Mathematics: The Art of Educated Guessing and Opportunistic Problem Solving.* MIT Press, ISBN 978-0-262-51429-3, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 (free PDF). Tools: dimensions, easy cases, lumping, pictorial proofs, taking out the big part, analogy. Preface: "Too much mathematical rigor teaches rigor mortis: the fear of making an unjustified leap"; "shoot first and ask questions later"; "A calculation accurate only to a factor of 2 may show that a proposed bridge would never be built." On fudging numbers: "75 divides easily into 3 and 300," and the fudge "might even cancel the lumping error." — [OAPEN PDF](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/26090/1/1003996.pdf)
- Mahajan, S. (2014). *The Art of Insight in Science and Engineering: Mastering Complexity.* MIT Press, ISBN 9780262526548, CC BY-NC-SA (free PDF on MIT OCW). Tools: divide and conquer; abstraction; symmetry and conservation; proportional reasoning; dimensions; lumping; probabilistic reasoning; easy cases; spring models. Grew out of his "Art of Approximation" course at Cambridge, MIT, and Olin. — [MIT OCW open textbook](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-011-the-art-of-insight-in-science-and-engineering-mastering-complexity-fall-2014/pages/open-textbook/)
- From the *Art of Insight* text: "Throughout this book, our goal is insight over accuracy"; "Approximations, I saw again, foster understanding"; "rigor easily becomes rigor mortis or paralysis by analysis." It uses g = 10 m s⁻² in worked estimates (e.g., 65 kg × 10 m s⁻² × 3.5 m ≈ 2000 J for climbing a flight of stairs), lists π ≈ 3 in its "values for backs of envelopes" table, uses factors of 3 and 10 with "3 × 3 ≈ 10," and argues for the geometric mean as the midpoint "on a ratio or logarithmic scale, which is the scale built into our mental hardware." — [MIT OCW textbook PDF](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-011-the-art-of-insight-in-science-and-engineering-mastering-complexity-fall-2014/mitres_6-011_f14_textbook.pdf)
- Mahajan (Physics Today Q&A, September 15, 2015): teaches estimating "to one-half of a significant figure" by rounding to "one" or "few" times a power of 10; estimation is improved by "teaching ratio reasoning" and "the courage to shoot first and ask questions later"; he organizes teaching by reasoning tools rather than physics topics; his goal is "to understand a lot of the world with only a little mathematics"; calculators "have been a disaster" for number sense. — [Physics Today](https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/questions-and-answers-with-sanjoy-mahajan)
- Redish, E. F., "Using math in physics: 2. Estimation" (series of teaching essays; arXiv:2011.12699). Practice: introduce estimation early and include it on every homework and exam; forbid look-ups and calculators; require one or two significant figures in powers of ten; require stated assumptions tied to trusted experience; grade the reasoning; accept answers within a factor of two or more. He says estimation builds a sense of scale and supports model building (deciding what to ignore), and counters the belief that physics is exact. No formal empirical study is reported. — [arXiv:2011.12699](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.12699)
- Empirical estimation research exists mainly in math and science education, e.g., a Münster study, "Students' measurement estimation skills and their relation to the process of solving Fermi problems." — [Münster CRIS record](https://cris-portal.uni-muenster.de/portal/en/publication/96627668) (not fetched; details unverified)

### Inferences
- The estimation tradition supports hard rounding (g = 10, π ≈ 3, "one or few") as a way to make the reasoning visible and the arithmetic mental. Mahajan's and Redish's grading rules (justified and roughly right gets full credit) are a ready model for assessment.
- Mahajan's tools (lumping, easy cases, proportional reasoning, dimensions) overlap with Arons's push for "arithmetic reasoning" over symbol-shuffling (§3) and with Karplus's proportional-reasoning work (§5). The estimation tradition is more developed for upper-level and engineering students than for absolute beginners.
- Rounding to "one or few" on a log scale is a principled version of "round hard": results that survive the rounding are the robust ones.

### Gaps
- I didn't find controlled studies showing that estimation-first teaching improves conceptual gains (FCI-type) in introductory physics; the evidence is practitioner experience and course design.
- The original Goldreich–Mahajan–Phinney course notes (often cited as "Order of Magnitude Physics: Understanding the World with Dimensional Analysis, Educated Guesswork, and White Lies," 1999) weren't located as a fetchable primary source; the subtitle and date are **unverified**.
- Redish's essay's publication venue wasn't stated in the fetched arXiv text.

## 5. Reform curricula rebuilt around a few principles (Matter & Interactions; Six Ideas; Modeling Instruction; Physics by Inquiry; Karplus)

### Takeaway
Each reform cut intro physics down to a small core: three fundamental principles plus an atomic model and computation (Matter & Interactions); conservation laws first, with Newton derived later (Six Ideas); a handful of student-built models in a modeling cycle (Modeling Instruction); concepts built from students' own observations, aimed at teachers (Physics by Inquiry); and the exploration–invention–application learning cycle (Karplus/SCIS). Modeling Instruction has the strongest FCI evidence and the widest high-school reach. Matter & Interactions shows a cautionary result: lower FCI gains, explained by content mismatch with the test. None of them made everyday-word-to-quantity mapping a central design principle.

### Cited Findings
**Matter & Interactions (Chabay & Sherwood).**
- Textbook editions: original 2002, 3rd 2011, 4th 2015; calculus-based; includes 20th-century physics throughout; emphasizes "the reductionist nature of physics." Chabay and Sherwood won AAPT's 2014 Halliday and Resnick Award. Sherwood helped develop VPython. — [Sherwood's NCSU page](https://physics.sciences.ncsu.edu/people/basherwo/)
- What it changes: mechanics organized around the momentum, energy, and angular momentum principles; de-emphasizes constant-acceleration kinematics; ball-and-spring atomic model of solids (elasticity, thermal energy, sound speed); circuits through surface charge; VPython programs that iterate the momentum principle. — [Kohlmyer et al., ASEE 2008, AC 2008-1166](https://per.gatech.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/ASEE2008.pdf)
- Georgia Tech outcomes: on the BEMA (E&M), M&I post-tests ran 55.9–59.8% versus 38.3–54.8% for traditional sections. On a Faraday's-law final-exam problem, 28% of M&I students were fully correct versus 17% traditional, and 15% versus 43% used a wrong principle. On the FCI (one section, Fall 2006), M&I ⟨g⟩ = 0.23 versus 0.34–0.48 traditional, which the authors called "particularly disappointing." By Spring 2008 about 30% of Georgia Tech's intro sequence (about 300 students per course) was in M&I. — [Kohlmyer et al. 2008](https://per.gatech.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/ASEE2008.pdf)
- Caballero et al., "Comparing large lecture mechanics curricula using the Force Concept Inventory: A five thousand student study" (Georgia Tech): traditional students gained more on nearly every FCI item, with the largest gaps in kinematics and force identification and the smallest on Newton's first law. The authors attribute this to content coverage (traditional homework covered FCI concepts about twice as much, 0.57 vs. 0.26) and conclude the FCI is "more closely aligned with traditional content." Matched pre/post means were 48.9%→71.3% traditional and 47.4%→59.3% M&I. I believe it appeared as *Am. J. Phys.* 80, 638 (2012), but the fetched text shows no journal reference, so that's **unverified**. — [arXiv:1106.1055](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1106.1055)

**Six Ideas That Shaped Physics (Thomas Moore, Pomona).**
- McGraw-Hill; first published 1998, now in a 4th edition; arose from the Introductory University Physics Project (1987–1996), which found college texts neglected 20th-century physics. Starts from conservation laws (with Noether's theorem as motivation) and presents Newtonian mechanics as following from symmetry. Units: C (conservation laws constrain interactions), N (the laws of physics are universal), R (frame-independent), E (electric and magnetic fields are unified), Q (particles behave like waves), T (some processes are irreversible). — [Wikipedia: Six Ideas that Shaped Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six_Ideas_that_Shaped_Physics); [McGraw-Hill Unit N page](https://www.mheducation.com/highered/product/six-ideas-that-shaped-physics-unit-n-moore.html)
- Adoption: Wikipedia says "widely adopted" without a source; Hope College is the only named adopter in the article. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six_Ideas_that_Shaped_Physics)

**Modeling Instruction (Hestenes, Wells, Swackhamer).**
- Wells, M., Hestenes, D., & Swackhamer, G. (1995). "A modeling method for high school physics instruction." *Am. J. Phys.* 63(7), 606–619. Content is organized around a small set of basic models (e.g., "particle subject to a constant force"); a modeling cycle of model development and model deployment; student whiteboards; the teacher as Socratic guide working from a misconceptions taxonomy. On the Mechanics Diagnostic, Wells's modeling class beat his own inquiry class by 19% and traditional classes by 15%. Early teacher workshops did much worse at first ("a sobering 4%" gain for the first group, 22% for the second): "the effectiveness of physics instruction depends heavily on the pedagogical expertise of the teacher." "Teaching by telling is ineffective." — [Wells et al. 1995 PDF](https://davidhestenes.net/modeling/R&E/ModelingMethod-Physics_1995.pdf)
- AMTA's summary of the Modeling Workshop Project (about 7,500 high-school students nationwide): FCI pre→post 26→42% traditional, 26→52% novice modelers, 29→69% expert modelers (undated). — [modelinginstruction.org, "How effective is modeling instruction?"](https://modelinginstruction.org/?p=12)
- PhysPort rates Modeling Instruction "Silver" ("the second highest level of research validation"), with demonstrated improvements in conceptual understanding, problem solving, lab skills, attitudes, retention, and success of underrepresented groups. AMTA runs three-week summer workshops nationwide. A U.S. Department of Education expert panel reviewed it (2000, 2001). — [PhysPort: Modeling Instruction](https://www.physport.org/methods/Modeling_Instruction); [AMTA publications page](https://www.modelinginstruction.org/effective/publications/)

**Physics by Inquiry (McDermott and the UW Physics Education Group).**
- McDermott, L. C., & the Physics Education Group at the University of Washington (1996). *Physics by Inquiry*, Vols. 1–2 (Wiley; a 3-volume series per PER Central), ISBN 0-471-14440-1 for Vol. 1. "Laboratory-based modules that provide a step-by-step introduction to physics and the physical sciences"; students start from their own observations of simple systems, work across representations, and build predictive models; designed and validated through research; aimed especially at pre-service and in-service K–12 teachers; "designed for courses focused on active student discovery rather than on memorization." — [ComPADRE record](https://www.compadre.org/portal/items/detail.cfm?ID=4337)

**Karplus (SCIS and the learning cycle).**
- Robert Karplus (1927–1990), a QED theorist turned science educator, directed the Science Curriculum Improvement Study (SCIS, a K–6 hands-on program) from 1961 to 1977; he extended Piagetian research to college students and adults, finding many had "important gaps" in abstract reasoning, especially in unfamiliar disciplines; his best-known proportional-reasoning task is "Mr. Tall–Mr. Short." AAPT president 1977; Oersted Medal 1980. — [Wikipedia: Robert Karplus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Karplus)
- The learning cycle originated in SCIS. Karplus and J. Myron Atkin described the "guided discovery" approach in 1962, and by 1967 it was "well established within the SCIS program." — [Zollman & Rebello, "Learning Cycles – Curricula Based on Research," 1998](https://web.phys.ksu.edu/papers/concepts/LCIntro.pdf)
- The learning cycle's exploration phase precedes concept introduction (the "invention" phase), and Wells et al. describe their modeling cycle's development stage as "exploration, invention, and concept introduction," which shows the lineage from Karplus. — [Wells et al. 1995](https://davidhestenes.net/modeling/R&E/ModelingMethod-Physics_1995.pdf)

### Inferences
- What each fixed: M&I fixed fragmentation (many formulas) by reducing mechanics to three principles and bridging to atoms and computation. Six Ideas fixed the omission of 20th-century physics and made conservation the starting point. Modeling fixed "teaching by telling" by having students build and defend a few models. Physics by Inquiry fixed the teacher-preparation gap with observation-first modules. Karplus fixed name-before-experience with the exploration → invention → application order (Arons's "idea first, name afterwards" is the same move).
- The M&I and FCI story is a caution for any principle-first curriculum: if a concept-test instrument tracks traditional topic coverage (kinematics, force identification), a reformed course can look worse on it. A new toolkit should either include the qualitative practice the FCI rewards or choose instruments matched to its own aims.
- None of these curricula made "choose plain-English words whose everyday sense matches the quantity" an organizing principle; Arons and Karplus come closest by ordering experience before naming. That's the clearest open niche relative to prior art.
- Modeling Instruction's teacher-expertise finding (the 4% first-workshop gain) suggests that a vocabulary toolkit's success may depend as much on how teachers use the words as on the words themselves.

### Gaps
- No reliable adoption counts found for M&I (number of institutions), Six Ideas, or Physics by Inquiry; the Wikipedia "widely adopted" claim for Six Ideas is unsourced.
- AMTA's teacher-trained totals weren't on the pages fetched (often quoted as several thousand teachers; **unverified**).
- The 5E model's descent from Karplus's learning cycle is commonly asserted (via BSCS), but no fetched source confirmed it.
- I didn't fetch primary outcome data for Six Ideas or Physics by Inquiry; the UW group's tutorial-based outcome data (e.g., *Tutorials in Introductory Physics*) is outside what was checked here.
