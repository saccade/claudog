DOG MATH
========

A dog: a doc written in dog math, about dog math. The rules are
doc's, from the ledger and from chat; the words are the model's.
Not doc's text.

1. Playing with ideas
---------------------

Dog math is about playing with ideas. When hands, cats,
lumps, thumps, pow and zoom are on the board, the work is real
science or real engineering, and it says out loud that it isn't
the last word. It won't get lost in the weeds, because the weeds
were rounded off at the gate.

Dog math is the default for all the work, unless doc says a piece
is for outside eyes (sections 14 and 16).

The job is naming. Find the quantity that means something, say
how to measure it or build it, and relate it to the others in
sentences that don't turn round on themselves. A lump of algebra,
or a sentence that tangles or runs long, means a quantity hasn't
been named yet: find the body or setup property it's reaching
for, name it, and let it eat the algebra. The quantity does the
structural work, and friendly units make the numbers easy.

2. House rules
--------------

 1. Round hard. Gravity is 10 zoom, dog gravity. Anything close
    to a round number is that number; a mole is 3/5 of a trillion
    trillion. A third digit is margin noise: 3.92 is 3.9, and
    0.533 is about 0.53.

 2. A result survives rounding, dropping, even scrambling, every
    digit past about the third. If 96 against 100 matters, you're
    measuring the wrong thing: pick a simpler example, or find
    the real quantity.

 3. Pick sizes so the units eat the algebra. Radii in whole hands
    make a whole number of cards, and where algebra seems to be
    needed a unit on the board usually eats it. Pick values that
    aren't halfway: a half hides which part is which.

 4. Local units are fine. A problem can define its own at the top
    when it makes the numbers whole: 1 mw is a milliwheel, so a
    lump at a hand, at a hand, is 10 mw.

 5. Every quantity gets a one-word unit: its own name, unless a
    named unit exists already. After a number you say "wheel",
    not "inertia". Force is both quantity and unit (five force,
    and the dog unit is the push); a new quantity gets the same
    deal.

 6. Dog units take no plural s: 5.3 hand, 7 lump, 14 push. 5.3
    hands is a trip to the hospital.

 7. Words mean what they mean. A name holds up against its
    dictionary meaning everywhere you can see it mapped; one name
    per thing, one thing per name. Assigning a word never bans
    its everyday sense: "spinning at 6" is still the sweep in
    plain English, so spin stays out of its way. Spin the
    quantity is the turning movement, inertia at sweep; the rate
    is the sweep. Where both are in play, say sweep or turning
    movement and leave the bare word out. Each word is a unit
    test on the algebra.

 8. The verbs are fixed by the old rules of the language. Energy
    is transformed. Work is performed. Punch and hook are
    delivered, or land. A spring returns what was put into it.
    Fuel is burned. Motion is traded, or added over time. A force
    does work along a path. Effort is taken, like a load: holding
    a load up takes effort and does no work. Spending is for
    energy, power and motion.

 9. Make the words work hard. A quantity can go adjectival (a
    ponderous bulk), adverbial, or get verbed (the drum picks
    up). Drop dead words, skip words, take shortenings, have fun
    with units, and don't police anyone's coinages. Formalize
    later, and only where it would step on toes.

10. Operators first. Assume crane and bulldozer operators
    understand this better than physicists, and adopt their
    language whenever possible.

11. Balanced statements, always. One-sided versions are for
    engineering.

12. What is simpler in turns is written in turns.

13. A letter means one thing in a problem, and never a unit's.
    Don't spend m on a phase with a metre on the page, or s on
    a swing beside seconds, or C on a charge beside a can. Give
    a phase φ and a swing θ, and leave the unit its letter.

14. Two observers are A and B: A is the active agent, the one
    who flashes and counts, and B is the subject, the one being
    watched. "A's own time" and "B's own time" say whose clock
    without a new letter, and "one's own" does it for anyone.
    With more than two, give names or numbers. (Not me and you:
    those are for the writer and the reader.)

15. Dog math says whatever the usual words say. It is those
    words plus relabelings, so it is never allowed to be less
    clear than they are. Where a usual name is clearer in the
    scene, say it, plainly and without comment: topspin and
    backspin, simple harmonic motion, the parallel-axis theorem,
    time dilation, angular momentum, event, frame. A name for a
    whole result is a handle, and a handle is just English.
    And where a dog word collides with something in the scene,
    the scene keeps its word and the dog word steps aside.

Where a dog word steps aside
----------------------------

Each of these is a rock on the trail. Point at it in passing, as
if obvious, and move on.

  - roll. In a problem with something rolling, the spin is the
    turning movement (or angular momentum), and its number goes
    in kg m^2/s. Roll stays a unit where nothing rolls.

  - wheel. Not as a unit in a problem about an actual wheel, nor
    for a rod or a pendulum. Use mw, or kg m^2.

  - forward and back around. Where the scene has a word for the
    sense of turning, topspin, backspin, the way a wheel rolls,
    use it (section 9).

  - outwardness and upwardness. They name parts along and across
    a start line, wherever it points. Say which way when it isn't
    up and out (section 9).

  - across. Only the product: a mass across a profile. A
    direction is sideways, level, or at right angles to.

  - run. Only a speed: it runs at 3 cat. A computer's work is a
    check, a whole motion is a motion.

  - frame, event. A frame is how places and times are counted. An
    event is a place at a time. Surface and floor are things you
    stand on.

  - moment. It has three jobs and the dog words keep them apart:
    the instant, the lever, the inertia.

  - punch, in a problem about a blow. The strike is a blow or a
    hit; punch is what it leaves in the centre's motion.

  - heft. Weight is the pull, heft is what gravity stores. The
    weight pulls; the height hefts.

  - swing. The angle swung through. Its biggest is the amplitude,
    and one trip out and back is a cycle.

  - can, in a problem with a can on the page. Ask "could", not
    "can": could the punch rise while the pace stays put?

  - coil. The coil is the wire and the store it makes. With a
    second coil in the story, such as the wire on a swinging rod,
    say which: the wire on the rod, the coil's store.

4. Saying products
------------------

Products and quotients take English operators, not ×, and get
verbed when the verbing's good.

    of       leads with the operator: the sweep of the lot, the
             capacitance of its voltage
    at       leads with the thing: the lot at pace, a mass at a
             lever
    across   a mass across a profile; a wheel across a zing
    through  a push through a distance
    on       divides: 3 roll on half a wheel
    over     divides, or runs along: 4 pickup over 4.5 stroke
    under    a bulk under gravitation

"Of" is the ordinary genitive, not a fixed operator. The product
is whichever the two kinds of object take: two axis arrows dot, an
axis arrow and a lever cross, a scale just scales. Two orders, two
words: "of" leads with the operator, "at" leads with the thing.
Running text uses the sentence that comes out positive.

Where "of" could be heard as belonging instead of times, and
the two hearings lead somewhere different ("the power is the
voltage of the current"), put the numbers beside it the first
time: 12 V of 2 A is 24 W. After that the bare rule is safe.

| Said                            | Is                            |
+---------------------------------+-------------------------------+
| 1 hand sweeping at 50           | pace, 5 cat                   |
| 7 lump moving at 7 cat          | movement, 49 oompf            |
| 2 oompf levered at 5 hand       | spin, 1 roll                  |
| 0.5 wheel sweeping at 2         | spin, 1 roll                  |
| 1.5 crank sweeping at 2         | power, 3 pow                  |
| 2 lump at 5 hand                | bulk, 1 ponder                |
| 18 zing of half a wheel         | hook, 9 thump                 |
| 2 lump across 2 card            | inertia, 40 mw                |

Duration and rate are rows in the multiplication table, not
columns. Say "z is the fluxion of y" (force is the fluxion of the
movement) and "y adds z over time" (a push adds movement over
time). Mass is handled by the ghost version beneath each cell.

5. Verbing
----------

A unit worth introducing has a verb in it, or if not, an adverb or
an adjective. The centre of verbing is what's happening.

  - A push pushes, and a pull pulls: 4 pull on 2 lump, steadily
    pulling at 4 along the ice. A crank cranks. A boost boosts.
    A wheel wheels: 0.27 wheel wheeling at 5.0 carries 1.35 roll.
    A body hooks, punches and hefts.

  - Movement and spin are carried: the cart carries 6 oompf, the
    wheel 3 roll. The ball gives the bat oompf, the blow boosts
    it, and the bat rolls with it.

  - Ponder is from the Latin for "to weigh". You ponder from a
    pivot: the rod ponders from P, and its bulk under gravity
    cranks it back. A ponderous bulk.

  - Whirl is the pickup of a body turning on itself, so it belongs
    where the body is in the sentence: 0.8 crank on 40 mw picks
    the puck up at 20 whirl. A train picking up round a track's
    centre picks up. A sentence with no body in it picks up too:
    20 pickup over 10 stroke adds 200 zing.

6. Ghosts
---------

A ghost body is a body with the mass taken out. A ghost still needs
something: a place to be, or some other property to have. Some
ghosts don't need to be anyplace in particular; they're sneaky
that way, though bodies get the same latitude (2 lump at 3 cat
doesn't say where). One can have only a velocity and a swirl. We
add places when there's more than one thing to keep apart. What a
ghost can't be is no matter, no place and no anything at all.
That's the ghost of a ghost, and it's just gone.

The centre of mass is the plainest ghost body: a place, a
velocity, a swirl about any line, and no mass of its own. A laser
dot is another.

A ghost quantity is a quantity with the body taken out. Velocity,
swirl and zip are a ghost's properties, the way movement, spin and
punch are a body's. Straight ghosts come per lump; turning ghosts
come per wheel. Some turning quantities also have a per-lump
ghost.

+--------------+-----------------------+-----------------------+
|              | per lump (mass out)   | per wheel (inertia    |
|              |                       | out)                  |
+--------------+-----------------------+-----------------------+
| oompf        | velocity              |                       |
| push         | acceleration          |                       |
| thump, punch | zip                   |                       |
| wheel        | profile               |                       |
| roll         | swirl                 | sweep                 |
| crank        | twirl                 | pickup                |
| thump, hook  | zump                  | zing                  |
| thump, work  | zug                   | zlug                  |
| pow          | zow                   | zlow                  |
+--------------+-----------------------+-----------------------+

Take the wheel out of a 2-lump ball at 5 hand, which is half a
wheel:

    3 roll on half a wheel sweeps at 6.
    2 crank on half a wheel picks up at 4.
    9 thump of hook on half a wheel is 18 zing.
    4 pickup over 4.5 stroke is 18 zlug.
    4 pickup at 6 sweep is 24 zlow.

Take the lump out of the same ball instead:

    half a wheel on 2 lump is 25 card of profile.
    9 thump of hook on 2 lump is 4.5 zump.
    25 card of profile across 18 zing is 4.5 zump.

Spin and torque per wheel leave the motion itself, sweep and
pickup, just as movement and push per lump leave the velocity and
the acceleration. Spin on the wheel gives the sweep wherever the
setup fixes the axis. A crank on the wheel gives the pickup only
while the inertia stays put: a skater tucking on a fixed axis
picks up with no crank at all.

Some ghosts we're so used to we've stopped seeing them: velocity,
speed, acceleration. One way to sort them: real quantities add,
ghosts average, each by its own weight, the mass for the per-lump
ghosts and the inertia for the per-wheel ones. Glue a 2-lump ball
at 3 cat to a 1-lump ball at 6 cat, both moving the same way:

    movement:  6 oompf and 6 oompf make 12 oompf. It adds.
    velocity:  3 cat and 6 cat make 4 cat, the 12 oompf spread
               across 3 lump. It averages, by the mass.

That's bookkeeping, not a rule about what an interaction changes.
Gluing is a collision. Movement comes through it, so the velocity
comes out as the mass-weighted average. Punch doesn't all come
through:

    before:  27 thump of punch, 9 zip
    after:   24 thump at 4 cat, 8 zip; 3 thump burned

Movement, spin, punch and hook are carried by bodies, and can be
split up and swapped around between them. Velocity, speed, zip and
acceleration belong to no body in particular, and split only by
the mass. Ghosts aren't weak for that. A free body keeps its
velocity, and velocities say where bodies will meet. What they
can't settle alone is how much motion gets traded when bodies
interact: that takes the masses and the rules of the interaction.

Dog gravity is a ghost: 10 pull a lump, 10 zoom. The mass is
already out, so everything falls together.

Ghosts carry no mass, so they walk through walls, and sometimes
they let you follow them if you dare. People get lost in those
walls. They say if you could see into the dark in there, it would
be nothing but algebra, everywhere; be grateful the lights are
off. The way out is always the same: put the body back across.
You go in after 18 zing, and on the far side half a wheel steps
out and hooks 9 thump. The ones who get lost forgot to bring the
mass back.

7. Verbing the ghosts
---------------------

A ghost doesn't have to sit after a number. Let it run. Put a
body, or a slice of one, across a ghost, and it takes the ghost's
verb:

    5 hand sweeping at 6 runs at 3 cat.
    Half a wheel sweeping at 6 hooks 9 thump.
    Half a wheel zinging at 18 hooks 9 thump.
    2 lump zipping at 2 punches 4 thump.
    1 lump dropping a hand hefts 1 thump.

Per lump, the profile takes the same verbs: 25 card sweeping at 6
hooks 4.5 thump a lump.

| Ghost       | Its verb       | Said                            |
+-------------+----------------+---------------------------------+
| velocity    | runs at        | the centre runs at 3 cat        |
| acceleration| speeds up at   | the block speeds up at 1.8 zoom |
| sweep       | sweeps at      | 5 hand sweeping at 6            |
| pickup      | picks up at    | the drum picks up at 18         |
| zip         | zips at        | 2 lump zipping at 2             |
| zing        | zings at       | half a wheel zinging at 18      |
| swirl       | swirls         | the ball swirls the same after  |
|             |                | the bounce as before            |
| twirl       | twirls         | a balanced wheel can twirl with |
|             |                | its centre still                |
+-------------+----------------+---------------------------------+

Ghosts take the passive voice too. The point is to pass them
around without anything in particular having them: 18 zing is
added, not "it gains 18 zing". Or let the quantity act: 4 pickup
over 4.5 stroke adds 18 zing. There's still a ghost body back
there, but it's rude to point at it. Put a body back across a
ghost and the active voice comes back with it: half a wheel with
18 zing hooks 9 thump.

Don't force the verbing. Where a verb's word hasn't come in yet,
the one that has will carry it: before zing is on the table,
"sweeping at" carries the hook. And round as you verb.

8. Ghost manners, and the letter
--------------------------------

Mention the ghosts enough that nobody is confused, then leave them
be. Most people are afraid of ghosts, so hold the reader's hand
and make no sudden moves.

  - Don't point. No possessives: "the rod's profile" becomes "the
    profile about the centre". No roll call at the end of each
    problem. No "here's another ghost": name the quantity and let
    it work. "Spin per mass we'll call the swirl."

  - Say what a ghost is once, plainly, where the reader first
    needs it: a quantity with the body's mass, or its inertia,
    taken out. After that the ghosts do their work quietly.

  - The familiar ones, velocity and the sweep, soothe, and need
    no letter. A problem with one scary ghost can give it a
    letter. That letter is Ω, the ghosts' true form, the base
    letter for the whole family: Ω for the one scary ghost, and
    Ω_profile, Ω_zing, Ω_zlow when several are in play. Marks
    stay (Ω₁, Ω₂, Ω₊, Ω₋); a different ghost gets a different
    subscript, and a repeated symbol is the same quantity.

  - The conventional quantities keep their usual symbols: I, K,
    ω, α, v and, in electricity, Z for impedance. The ghosts give
    way to them, never the other way round. Ω the ghost never
    shares an expression with the ohm: quantity algebra and unit
    statements stay separate.

  - All the ghosts wear Ω, or none do. Nobody gets left visible
    alone. If a later room needs the switch, the earlier rooms
    change with it.

9. Turning words
----------------

A stroke is a radian. A turn is about 6.3 stroke, and what is
simpler in turns is written in turns. The quadrant and the flip
are a quarter and a half of it.

The two senses of turning are forward around (the default) and
backward around. Say them in full on the first mention in each
problem, like each new newspaper story. After that forward around
shortens to "around"; backward around never does, but "back
around" is fine. Widdershins and sunwise can be mentioned once,
where they are defined.

Where the scene has its own word for the sense, use it. Forward
around is the way a point goes widdershins; it is not the way a
ball travels. A ball rolling to the right turns back around,
which is topspin, and a blow below its centre starts it turning
forward around, which is backspin.

Arrows get their parts named, in plain composition. A 5 cat run
up a 3-4-5 slope has an upwardness of 3 cat and an outwardness of
4 cat. Divide the arrow by up and the 3 comes out; by out, the 4.
Say upwardness, how much of the way up, fall, lift. Work with the
parts. When the swing is the variable, the parts have names too:
the upwardness of a swing and the outwardness of a swing are the
parts of an arrow one long, swung forward around from straight
out. A 5 cat run swung 0.64 stroke has 5 upwardness of 0.64, 3 cat
up, and 5 outwardness of 0.64, 4 cat out. So a bead R from the
centre, swung s from straight out, sits R outwardness of s across
and R upwardness of s up.

The parts belong to the start line, not the compass. Outwardness
is the part along the start line and upwardness the part across
it, wherever it points. A bead swung s from straight down sits
R outwardness of s down and R upwardness of s sideways: say
"down" and "sideways" with them. A wave has no start line; its
outwardness and upwardness of the phase are the two shapes a
quarter turn apart.

A body turning has a sweep. A swing or a wave also has a ring, a
stroke of phase a second: a pendulum sweeps when it moves, and
rings at its own rate when it swings.

A swing at one ring and no other is a tone (on trial): the tone
rings at its ring. It is what is called simple harmonic motion: a
pickup of minus the ring, squared, across the swing. Use either
name; the old one is a handle for the whole result. A system with
two ways to ring on its own has two tones, each with its own ring;
both at once is a chord, and the chord beats at the gap between
its rings. The two tones of a rod coupled to a loop, say, ring at
1.6 and 3.6, and beat at 2.0.

10. Balanced, with the d's on different sides
---------------------------------------------

Write the balanced version: both sides of every push, both
ledgers, stated positive direction.

Put the differentials on different sides of the balance. They can
divide out either way, they're not picky, and then you're not
futzing with fractions. Read both layouts aloud, and use the one
that tells the physical transfer: what acts, what moves through
what, and what is stored.

4 pull on a 2-lump block on smooth ice, for 2 seconds:

    F dt = dp         4 pull across 2 s adds 8 oompf
                      2 lump with 8 oompf runs at 4 cat

    F dx = d(½ m v²)  4 pull through 4 metre adds 16 thump of
                      punch: 2 lump at 4 cat punches 16 thump

The rate form, dK/dt = F v, throws away most of the information
and compresses the answer. The d's apart say who pushes, how far
it moves, and what is kept.

Exchange rates (on trial)
-------------------------

When the change is more than a plain rate, or both sides are
crowded, usually (not always) break it out. Ask what is really
being carried, from what to where, and at what exchange rate. A
store changes by the rate across the change in what is carried,
one term for each thing carried:

    carried        rate       the store changes by
    oompf          pace       pace across d(oompf)
    roll           sweep      sweep across d(roll)
    metre          pull       pull across d(metre)
    stroke         crank      crank across d(stroke)
    charge         voltage    voltage across d(charge)
    linked flux    current    current across d(flux)

The rate is thump per unit carried: a pace is thump per oompf,
1 cat, so the units check themselves. A plain rate stays a plain
rate; break out only when the plain version hides the exchange.

Each body has its own rate. A 2 lump at 3 cat meets a 1 lump at
rest and they stick. The 2 lump hands over 2 oompf, at paces
running from 3 down to 2 cat: it loses 5 thump. The 1 lump takes
the same 2 oompf at paces from 0 up to 2: it gains 2 thump. The
3 thump between is the burn, the gap between the two paces summed
over the oompf swapped.

When the carrying changes the rate itself, there is a second term
for it. Roll kept and the wheel shrinking: the hook rises by half
a zing across the wheel lost. That is the skater drawing her arms
in; the hook she gains is what her arms spent.

11. Electricity
---------------

    Current is the conductance of the voltage:   I = G V
    Voltage is the resistance of the current:    V = R I
    Power is the voltage of the current:         P = V I

A resistor burns power, turning electrical energy into heat.
Current is charge's fluxion; voltage is energy per charge. "Of"
supplies the multiplication; "fluxion" supplies the rate of
change.

Can and coil are good strong words. They work as nouns, verbs,
gerunds, all kinds of things. A can cans charge; a coil coils
flux.

The can cans the separated charge, Q.
The charge a can cans is the capacitance of its voltage:

    Q = C V

The coil coils the linked flux, λ. A coil is a helix: the wire
goes N turns around the axis, stepping along it as it goes, so
the flux through one turn counts N times. The flux a coil coils
is the inductance of its current:

    λ = N Φ = L I

The coil gets the rule that mirrors the can's. Current is
charge's fluxion; voltage is linked flux's fluxion:

    I = dQ/dt,     V = dλ/dt

A can stores the voltage of added charge. A coil stores the
current of added flux. Same move:

    dE = V dQ,     dE = I dλ

From empty, the voltage rises with the charge and the current
rises with the flux, so each store fills the triangle under its
line, half the rectangle, the way punch does:

    E_can = ½ Q V,     E_coil = ½ λ I

Every can sentence becomes a coil sentence by trading charge for
flux and voltage for current:

+-----------------------------------+-----------------------------------+
| The can                           | The coil                          |
+-----------------------------------+-----------------------------------+
| cans charge, Q                    | coils linked flux, λ              |
| at a voltage, V                   | at a current, I                   |
| capacitance, C                    | inductance, L                     |
| current is charge's fluxion       | voltage is flux's fluxion         |
| cans the capacitance of its       | coils the inductance of its       |
| voltage: Q = C V                  | current: λ = L I                  |
| stores the voltage of added       | stores the current of added flux: |
| charge: dE = V dQ                 | dE = I dλ                         |
| holds ½ Q V                       | holds ½ λ I                       |
+-----------------------------------+-----------------------------------+

Verbing them right:

  - A can cans charge, at a voltage. It doesn't can voltage: the
    voltage is what it's at. "The can cans 24 mC at 12 V."

  - A coil coils flux, at a current. It doesn't coil current: the
    current runs around the turns, and the flux is what threads
    them. "The coil coils 1.2 Wb at 3.0 A."

  - A resistor burns power. It cans and coils nothing.

  - A coil is the wire and the store it makes. The wire on a
    swinging rod is the wire on the rod; the coil is what it
    stores.

  - The rules carry the verbs for the rest: current is the
    conductance of the voltage, voltage the resistance of the
    current, power the voltage of the current, and the store the
    voltage of added charge or the current of added flux.

Two problems, said strong
-------------------------

The can on the battery

    A 12 V battery charges an empty 2000 µF can through a
    150 Ω resistor. How much charge does the can can, how fast
    does the current start, how long does it take, and where
    does the energy go?

At the start the can is empty and at 0 V, so the resistor takes
the whole 12 V. The current is the conductance of 12 V across
150 Ω: 0.080 A.

The can cans charge until it is at the battery's 12 V. It cans the
capacitance of 12 V, 2000 µF of 12 V: 24 mC. The resistor and the
can together set the time, 150 Ω across 2000 µF, 0.30 s: the can
cans about two thirds of its charge in the first 0.30 s and 95
percent of it by 0.90 s.

The battery hands over the voltage of the charge it moves, 12 V of
24 mC: 0.29 J. The can stores the voltage of added charge, and
that voltage climbs from 0 to 12 V as the charge comes in, so the
can keeps half: the triangle, not the rectangle. The resistor
burns the other half. Half and half whatever the resistor: a
bigger one takes longer and burns just as much.

The coil that won't let go

    A 0.40 H coil carries a steady 3.0 A. A switch opens, and the
    coil's only path is through a 10 Ω resistor. How much flux
    does it coil, what voltage appears, how long does it last,
    and where does the energy go?

The coil coils the inductance of its 3.0 A, 0.40 H of 3.0 A:
1.2 Wb. The switch opens, and the current can't stop at once.
Voltage is flux's fluxion, and dropping 1.2 Wb in no time at all
would take a voltage without limit. So the coil keeps the 3.0 A
going, through the resistor. The voltage is the resistance of the
current, 10 Ω of 3.0 A: 30 V. To make it, the coil lets go of flux
at 30 Wb a second.

As the current falls, the voltage falls with it. The coil and the
resistor set the time, 0.40 H over 10 Ω, 0.040 s: the same two
thirds gone in the first 0.040 s, 95 percent by 0.12 s.

The coil stored the current of added flux, and that current
climbed from 0 to 3.0 A as the 1.2 Wb came in: half the rectangle,
1.8 J. The resistor burns all of it. At the start it burns the
voltage of the current, 30 V of 3.0 A, 90 W, and the coil's store
falls at the same 90 W: the current of 30 Wb a second of flux
going out.

Hold the two problems side by side and they're one problem in the
mirror. The can took charge in, filling at the voltage; the coil
lets flux out, emptying at the current.

The electrical units stay in their own names (V, A, C, F, H, Wb),
and the ohm is spelled out wherever the ghost letter Ω is also
on the page. The dog park has no volt of its own.

A pattern that slides
---------------------

A line has a coil and a can in every metre: L_line H and C_line F.
Across dt, the voltage dropped along a short dx is the coil's,
and the current that goes in at the left and doesn't come out at
the right is the can's:

    (V_left − V_right) dt = L_line dx dI
    (I_left − I_right) dt = C_line dx dV

Ask what it takes for a pattern to slide right at u without
changing shape. Across dt the place at x shows what was u dt to
its left, so dx dV = u dt (V_left − V_right), and the same for
the current. Put that into the two balances. The drops can't both
hold unless L_line u across C_line u is 1:

    u = 1 on √(L_line across C_line)

and the voltage drop is then L_line u of the current drop. That
is the impedance Z, √(L_line on C_line). A pattern sliding right
carries I = V on Z; one sliding left, I = −V on Z.

Any state of the line splits that way. V₊ is (V + Z I) over 2,
and slides right; V₋ is (V − Z I) over 2, and slides left. They
put back V = V₊ + V₋ and Z I = V₊ − V₋. Each wave moves its own
C_line V² a metre at u, so its power is V² on Z, and nothing
steers either one faster than u.

Both waves at +6.0 V on a 50 ohm line: V = 12 V, I = 0. Nothing
flows past, and nothing is sitting still: 0.72 W goes right in
V₊ and 0.72 W goes left in V₋.

12. Light and clocks (on trial)
-------------------------------

Two ships coast apart at a steady pace: A, the active agent, and
B, the subject. Each has a clock, and the one thing they can both
count is light. A flashes on every tick of A's clock; B sees the
flashes arrive. They arrive spaced out by one fixed number, the
same on every tick: the shift, s. Going away, the flashes land s
times farther apart and the ring is cut by s. Coming at you, they
land s times closer and the ring is s times up. By symmetry each
gets the same s from the other, and light keeps its own pace for
both. That is all it takes. (It is Hermann Bondi's way in
Relativity and Common Sense, which builds the whole subject on
this one number.)

What the shift gives, with A and B side by side at time 0:

  - Pace. A flashes at time t on A's clock. B, going away, sees
    it at s t on B's clock, bounces it, and A sees the bounce at
    s s t. The bounce happened halfway between: at A's time
    (1 + s s) t / 2, and at a distance of light's reach across
    half the wait, c (s s − 1) t / 2. B's pace is the distance
    on the time:

        v = c (s s − 1) on (s s + 1)

  - Stretch. The same bounce is s t on B's clock and
    (1 + s s) t / 2 on A's. A's time on B's is the stretch:

        stretch = (s + 1 on s) / 2

    the mean of the shift and its reciprocal. Stretch across the
    pace on light is half their gap, (s − 1 on s) / 2. At s = 2:
    pace 3/5 c, stretch 5/4, and 3/4 for stretch across pace.
    B's clock, going away, reads less than A's by the stretch;
    lengths along the motion shrink by the same number.

  - Pace adds by multiplying. Two hops of 3/5 c are shifts 2
    and 2, so s = 4, and the pace is c (16 − 1) on (16 + 1),
    15/17 c, 0.88 c. No formula for adding paces is needed.

  - Light's energy rides its ring, so it takes the shift. Seen
    from someone going right at 3/5 c, a pulse running right is
    cut to half and a pulse running left is doubled: 80 thump
    becomes 40 and 20 becomes 40. The two sides multiply to 1,
    which is what it means that the transformation undoes itself.

An event is a place at a time. Frames give an event different
places and different times, but they agree on one thing: the time
a clock reads when it is carried from one event to the other, its
own time. The receiver's own time is 15.6 s, in every frame. S
counts 19.5 s, the stretch times more, and the 3.9 s between is
lost time, lost only in the counting: from the receiver's side it
is S's clock that reads less. In H, where the receiver sits still,
nothing is lost.

Check: in S, c across 19.5 s is 5.85×10⁹ metres and the receiver
goes 3.5×10⁹ of them, and 5.85² less 3.5² is 4.7², the same as c
across the own time, 15.6 s. In H, the distance is 0.

13. Stealth mode
----------------

For dogs who need to pass. Put muscle and skin back on the
close-to-the-bone problems. The dog units stay home (SI, with
oompf, roll, ponder and wheel), and the vocabulary comes in
quietly. Keep the strong language as if it's no big deal: the
verb is the argument, slipped past before anyone notices the math
is over.

  - Start each problem in the textbook's terms, and end each part
    a little further in. It's "Mary had a little lamb" changing
    key: the same openings, and the endings reveal the new key.

  - Introduce a unit once, in the textbook's terms: "0.072 kg m^2,
    which we'll call 0.072 wheel". Then just use it: the rod
    wheels at 15.

  - Every unit that comes in gets a verb, an adverb or an
    adjective: wheel wheels, crank cranks, hook hooks, a bulk
    ponders from its pivot.

  - A few new names per problem, each in its own sentence, never
    inside the hardest sentence in the room.

  - Read it aloud. If you're chewing through "angular
    acceleration" so often that when another shows up you can
    still taste the last one, it's time to pick up the pace.

  - Introduce the word when the reader is ready to use it. Say the
    textbook's phrase once in full, and let the sentence start
    asking for the name, so "we'll call it the pickup" lands as
    relief. Then it earns its keep at once: the drum picks up,
    picks up faster, stops picking up.

  - Move off sine and cosine and into the arrows by the third
    problem (section 9).

  - Don't point at the ghosts (section 8).

The ball that skids, road-legal

    A uniform solid ball, 1.0 kg and 0.050 m in radius, lies at
    rest on a level surface. A sharp horizontal blow above the
    centre sends it off at 2.1 m/s with topspin. Friction,
    μ = 0.20, then drags it to rolling, at 9/7 of 2.1 m/s. How
    high was the blow, how long is the skid, and how much energy
    does it burn?

Profile first: the ball's mass is laid out the same way at any
size, and its profile about the centre is (2/5) R^2 = 0.0010 m^2,
0.10 card. With the 1.0 kg back across it, the inertia is
0.0010 kg m^2.

Friction acts at the contact and has no lever about the contact
line, and gravity and the surface's push cancel there, so the
angular momentum about that line holds from the blow to the roll.
Once rolling, the ball turns about its contact, with profile
(7/5) R^2 = 0.0035 m^2, sweeping at (2.7 m/s) / R = 54 rad/s:
0.19 roll. Before, the centre's 2.1 oompf levered at R is
0.105 roll, plus the spin, 0.0010 kg m^2 at some ω0. The two
agree when ω0 is 84 rad/s, topspin.

The blow's angular impulse about the centre is the spin it
leaves, 0.0010 × 84 = 0.084 roll. The blow itself is 2.1 oompf,
so it landed 0.084 on 2.1, 0.040 m above the centre: h = 4R/5.

The skid. The bottom of the ball slides backward at
2.1 m/s - 84 × 0.050 = -2.1 m/s, so friction pulls forward,
μ m g = 2.0 N, which speeds the centre up at 2.0 m/s^2: from 2.1
to 2.7 m/s takes 0.30 s, and the centre goes 2.4 × 0.30 = 0.72 m.
Check from the other side: 2.0 N at R is a torque of 0.10 N m on
0.0010 kg m^2, 100 rad/s^2, and 84 down to 54 takes 0.30 s.

The energy. Just after the blow the centre punches
(1/2)(1.0)(2.1)^2 = 2.2 J, and the spin, at 3500 zing, hooks
0.0010 × 3500 = 3.5 J: 5.7 J. Rolling, it punches 3.6 J and
hooks 1.5 J at 1500 zing: 5.1 J. The skid burned 0.63 J, about a
ninth. The same from the friction: 2.0 N times the 0.315 m the
contact slides, an average of 1.05 m/s for 0.30 s, is 0.63 J.

17. A few problems, said strong
-------------------------------

Three of the working problems, cut down and said the way the
words like to be used.

The drum picks up

    A wheel turns freely on its axle: a uniform 2-lump disc 2 hand
    in radius, with a uniform 1-lump drum of 1 hand fixed to it on
    the same axle. A rope
    off the drum runs over a smooth post and down to a 1-lump
    block. Let go from rest, how long does the block take to fall
    a metre?

    The profiles go in cards. The disc has half of 4 card, 2 card;
    the drum half of 1 card. The rope makes the block's fall load
    the axle like a lump on the drum's rim, so the block presents
    1 card at the drum. With the mass back across:

        2 lump across 2 card       40 mw
        1 lump across half a card   5 mw
        1 lump across 1 card       10 mw
        the setup                  55 mw

    The block's bulk on the drum is 1 lump at 1 hand, 0.1 ponder.
    Under 10 zoom it cranks at 1. 1 crank on the whole 55 mw picks
    it up at 18, a little over, and 1 hand picking up at 18 speeds
    the block up at 1.8 zoom. A metre from rest takes a second and
    a bit, 1.05; the block lands running at 1.9 cat, the wheel
    sweeping at 19.

    The fall lets go 10 thump of heft, and sweeping at 19 adds 180
    zing. 55 mw with 180 zing hooks 10 thump: the disc and drum's
    45 mw hooks 8.2, the block's 10 mw the other 1.8. The rope
    carries 10 pull less the 1.8 the block keeps, so 8.2 pull
    through the metre puts 8.2 thump into the wheel. Check: 8.2
    pull at 1 hand cranks the wheel's own 45 mw at 0.82, and that
    picks it up at 18 too.

The ball that skids

    A uniform solid bowling ball, 8 lump and 1 hand in radius,
    leaves the hand at 7 cat with no sweep at all. While it skids,
    the lane drags at 16 push. How fast is it going once it rolls?

    Set it up about a line across the lane through the launch
    contact. The drag has no leverage about that line, and the
    weight's and the floor's leverages cancel, so the spin holds.
    8 lump at 1 hand is 0.8 ponder; moving at 7 cat, it has 5.6
    roll, backward around. From here on, back around.

    Moving the line along the lane to the contact once it rolls
    adds no leverage on the movement, so the spin stays 5.6 roll.
    Rolling, the ball turns about its contact: 2/5 card of its own
    profile and 1 card for the centre, 7/5 card. 8 lump across
    7/5 card is 112 mw. 5.6 roll on 112 mw sweeps at 50, and 1 hand
    sweeping at 50 runs at 5 cat. It keeps 5/7 of its pace.

    16 push on 8 lump slows it at 2 zoom, so 7 cat down to 5 takes
    a second; averaging 6, it skids 6 metres. 8 lump moving at
    7 cat punches 196 thump; rolling, 112 mw with 1250 zing hooks
    140. The slip falls steadily from 7 cat to nothing, and 16
    push through the 3.5 metres it rubs burns the other 56. It
    travels 6 metres and rubs 3.5.

The puck on a string

    A uniform 2-lump puck, 2 hand in radius, lies at rest on smooth
    ice with string wound on its rim, leaving on the puck's right.
    A hand steadily pulls at 4, straight ahead along the ice, for
    a second. How far does the hand go, and how much of its work
    ends up as punch?

    Each ledger gets the whole pull. 4 pull on 2 lump speeds the
    centre up at 2 zoom. 4 pull at 2 hand cranks at 0.8, and 0.8
    crank on 40 mw (2 lump across 2 card) picks it up at 20,
    forward around. From here on, around.

    After the second the centre runs at 2 cat, a metre along;
    sweeping at 20, the puck has swung through 10 stroke and paid
    2 metres of string off the rim. The hand went 3.

    As ghosts: 2 zoom over a metre adds 2 zip, and 20 pickup over
    10 stroke adds 200 zing. With the bodies back: 2 lump with
    2 zip punches 4 thump, and 40 mw with 200 zing hooks 8. 4 pull
    through 3 metres adds 12 thump, all of it.

    Punch's share is the profile over the profile and the radius's
    reflex: 2 card over 6 card, a third.
