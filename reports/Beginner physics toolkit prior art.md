# Every field fixed its own piece

Written by Claude (Anthropic), 2026-10-25.

Reading taken: a briefing that maps each piece of the toolkit to the fields that already use it, says what's missing once the pieces are put together, and lays out why chatbots make it urgent.

kraemer's claim holds up: every piece of his proposed toolkit is already working practice somewhere, usually for decades, and no curriculum assembles them for beginners. Turns and cycle counts run GNSS positioning, interferometry, and rotating-machine specs. The single number that's both a motor's torque constant and its back-EMF constant is ordinary datasheet physics. Electron flow with left-hand rules was US Navy doctrine by 1949. Rapidity dates to 1910–11, and Bondi wrote the Doppler-factor version up for lay readers in 1964. Estimation-first teaching has a Caltech course and three Mahajan books behind it. What each field left behind is the joints: each keeps a private conversion (a 2π, a patched minus sign, a swapped hand) at its own boundary, and nobody has checked that the pieces agree when they're used together. The reform history is sobering. Hestenes's geometric algebra stalled on institutional walls, rapidity was dropped from at least one major relativity text, and Matter & Interactions scored lower on the Force Concept Inventory, while the reform that spread, Modeling Instruction, kept standard notation and gave teachers workshops and a shared test. The chatbot evidence sharpens the case but narrows it: 2026 models are A students on text-based intro physics, unguarded use measurably lowers later unaided scores, and the errors models still make cluster on directions, diagrams, and right-hand-rule procedures. Models also do algebra well, though, so a checkable toolkit helps mainly where checking happens live. None of the pieces has been tested head to head in a classroom, which is both the gap and the opening.

## Every piece has a home field, and most homes are old

The map below lists each proposal (all of them posited and under test, not settled) next to the field that already owns it.

| Piece (kraemer's proposal) | Where it already lives | Since | What that field fixed | What it left out |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Angles and phases in turns | GNSS carrier phase, interferometry, InSAR, machine specs (ISO "revolution"), HP calculators' "tr" mode, τ constants in Python, Rust, and .NET | Meter as a wavelength count, 1960; Hoyle's milliturns, 1962; Palais, 2001; Hartl, 2010; Python 3.6, 2016 | Whole counts plus a measured fraction; no `2*pi` everywhere | Each field keeps a private conversion (λ, h vs ħ, 2π); `math.tau` is just a float |
| Radian as a real unit (the stroke) | Metrology dissenters: Brinsmade, Brownstein, Quincey, Mohr and Phillips, Leonard | 1936 onward | Torque × angle = energy; Hz and rad/s kept apart | Never adopted; SI wording revised in 2024 with no change in status |
| Capture κ | Motor torque and back-EMF constants, loudspeaker Bl, hydraulic displacement | Long-standing engineering practice | One number read both ways (in SI) | Datasheets split it (rpm/V, peak vs RMS, factors of 3/2); no shared name across domains; the angle unit stays hidden |

### Turns and capture already run instruments, standards, and datasheets

Wherever a field counts, it counts turns. GNSS receivers write carrier phase as range plus a whole number of cycles times the wavelength, measure only the fraction at lock-on, and lose the integer in a "cycle slip" ([NPTEL GPS module](https://archive.nptel.ac.in/content/storage2/courses/105104100/lectureB_12/B_12_6carrier.htm)). From 1960 to 1983 the meter was literally **1 650 763.73 wavelengths** of a krypton line ([BIPM](https://www.bipm.org/committees/cg/cgpm/11-1960/resolution-6)). A Sentinel-1 radar fringe is about **2.8 cm** of ground motion ([Copernicus](https://www.copernicus.eu/en/node/5973)), and semiclassical quantization writes ∮p dq in whole units of h, with turning-point corrections counted in quarter cycles ([Rau](https://arxiv.org/abs/0906.4871)). Radians show up mostly where someone is doing calculus. The τ movement (Palais in 2001, Hartl's manifesto in 2010) got τ shipped as a constant in Python 3.6, Rust, .NET, and Unity ([PEP 628](https://peps.python.org/pep-0628); [Rust docs](https://doc.rust-lang.org/std/f64/consts/constant.TAU.html); [dotnet issue](https://github.com/dotnet/runtime/issues/24678)), but what got adopted was τ as a number, still meaning "radians in a turn." The turn as a unit had a separate life in ISO 80000-3, German standards, and HP calculators that never needed the campaign ([Wikipedia, "Turn (angle)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_(angle))). The critics' main points were that diameter is easier to measure, that A = πr² is tidier, and that statistics formulas pick up halves; the symbol clash (τ for torque, time constants, torsion) is the one a turn-first text has to settle out loud ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-some-mathematicians-think-we-should-abandon-pi/)).

Treating the radian as a real unit puts kraemer's stroke in a respectable minority. The SI has called it a dimensionless derived unit that "may, but need not" be written since 1995 ([BIPM, CGPM 1995](https://www.bipm.org/en/committees/cg/cgpm/20-1995/resolution-8)). A line of metrologists argues that angle deserves its own dimension, so that torque carries J/rad and **torque × angle = energy**, and so that Hz and rad/s stop hiding a factor of 2π ([Quincey and Brown](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.02373); [Quincey 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.05704)). Mathematicians answer that angle measure is a pure number ([Grötschel et al.](https://www.zib.de/groetschel/pubnew/geometry-opinion-comment.pdf)). The 2021 meeting of the Consultative Committee for Units split, with six bodies for the status quo and NIST and IUPAP for a new base unit ([CCU25 report](https://www.bipm.org/documents/20126/68201438/CCU25.pdf/ab1be833-d656-ae2e-36fa-feb04948ed4c)); its task group was dissolved in May 2024, and the August 2024 brochure revision **changed wording, not status**, while urging explicit units ([BIPM news](https://bipm.org/en/-/2024-09-11-si-brochure-301)). Nothing suggests a change before the next edition, around 2030. The student evidence that the invisible radian hurts is a 2025 preprint: of 769 students in four countries, only **26.3%** knew the standard trig derivatives hold only in radians ([Ben-Zion et al., arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01525)). Even Redish, who says we probably should give angle a dimension, advises radians-as-one for students on practical grounds ([Redish, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.12760)), so a turns-first course would be ahead of current teaching practice, not following it. One practical warning for the check scripts: Pint treats angle as dimensionless and converts 60 rpm to about 6.28 Hz instead of 1 Hz ([Pint docs](https://pint.readthedocs.io/en/latest/user/angular_frequency.html)), so the notebooks need to declare angle as its own dimension rather than trust the library's default.

Capture is the strongest case of a piece that already exists without a shared name. Power balance (e·i = χ·ω, torque χ times turn speed ω) forces a permanent-magnet motor's torque constant and back-EMF constant to be the same number in SI; an MIT course handout measures **0.0355 N·m/A and 0.0355 V·s/rad** and calls them "in fact identical" ([Hogan, MIT OCW](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-141-modeling-and-simulation-of-dynamic-systems-fall-2006/c91a0747722f114e7d2725fae73fafa3_dcpmm_basics.pdf)). Manufacturers already quote the speed constant in rpm per volt, which is turns in a datasheet ([maxon](https://support.maxongroup.com/hc/en-us/articles/360005873794-Motor-constants)), and real datasheets disagree by factors of √2 (peak vs RMS), line-to-line vs phase voltage, and 3/2 in some three-phase conventions ([Solo Motor Controllers](https://www.solomotorcontrollers.com/blog/back-emf-constant-vs-torque-constant/)), so a beginner who meets a real motor will find capture split in two. The loudspeaker force factor Bl, in T·m = N/A = V·s/m, is the same coupling per meter ([My New Microphone](https://mynewmicrophone.com/full-list-thiele-small-speaker-parameters-w-descriptions/)), and in real drivers it varies with cone position ([Klippel](https://www.klippel.de/know-how/measurements/transducer-parameters/transducer-nonlinearities-curve-shape.html)). Hydraulics has the fluid twin: theoretical motor torque is pressure drop times displacement per revolution over 2π, and pumps and motors are often interchangeable ([Burks, U. Florida](https://abe.ufl.edu/faculty/tburks/Presentations/ABE4171/Hydraulic%20Systems_2.pdf)). That's displacement per stroke in kraemer's unit, which is worth a glance, since in hydraulics and engines "stroke" already means piston travel.

The hoist trade supplies the words for running the coupling backward. An overhauling load pulls the rotor faster than the field and "turns the motor into a generator"; regenerative braking returns that energy to the line, dynamic braking dumps it in resistors, and eddy-current brakes "only retard" a lowering load because "retarding torque is only produced with motion" ([Hoist magazine](https://www.hoistmagazine.com/analysis/braking-holding-and-controlling/); [Plant Services](https://www.plantservices.com/articles/2006/023); [Konecranes](https://www.konecranes.com/discover/the-breakdown-holding-brakes-vs-mechanical-load-brakes-vs-dynamic-load-brakes)). That last line is drag that cares about speed, in an operator's own words.

### Direction and charge each got patched in one corner

Franklin's 1747 letter to Collinson introduced "positively or plus" and "negatively or minus" ([Founders Online](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Franklin/01-03-02-0072)), and the assignment was an accident: rubbed glass loses electrons ([Jensen, J. Chem. Educ.](https://homepages.uc.edu/~jensenwb/reprints/125.%20Positive%20&%20Negative.pdf)). Current was defined before anyone knew what moved in a wire ([IOP Spark](https://spark.iop.org/many-students-are-confused-idea-conventional-current)), and semiconductor symbols now lock conventional arrows into every diode and transistor ([Lessons in Electric Circuits](https://ecstudiosystems.com/discover/textbooks/lessons/DC/chapter1-7-conventional-versus-electron-flow/)). The Navy went the other way. Its 1949 *Electrician's Mate 3* says flow runs "from NEGATIVE to POSITIVE" and teaches left-hand rules for conductors and coils ([RF Cafe, NAVPERS 10548](https://www.rfcafe.com/references/electrical/electricians-mate-3-navpers-10548/navy-training-electricians-mate-3-NAVPERS-10548-chapter-3.htm)); the later NEETS modules carry a left-hand generator rule ([NEETS Module 5](https://www.rfcafe.com/references/electrical/neets-modules/NEETS-Module-05-1-1-1-10.htm)); and publishers still print an electron-flow edition of Floyd's circuits text ([UNM Bookstore](https://bookstore.unm.edu/p-98860-principles-of-electric-circuits-electron-flow-version.aspx)). The tradition's known weakness, in one experienced engineer's forum opinion, is that "virtually no one that uses electron flow does it properly," patching in unexplained minus signs ([All About Circuits forum](https://forum.allaboutcircuits.com/threads/neets-manual-defintions.171305/)). Both traditions make the same physical predictions, because a mirror flip of the hand cancels a flip of the charge sign.

The formal backing for the hidden backward arrow is charge-flip symmetry: flip every charge-borne quantity (q, ρ, J, E, B, the potentials) and Maxwell's equations and the Lorentz force come back unchanged ([Torromé, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1403.5141); [Kotel'nikov, arXiv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/quant-ph/9707003)). R = V/I, C = Q/V, and L = λ/I are each a ratio of two flipped quantities, so they don't change. That follows straight from the definitions, and no teaching text found says it to students. One consistency check the scripts should carry: capture, being a flux linkage, flips with the charges, while torque and power, its products with current, don't. Geometric algebra attacks the same corner from geometry, treating the axial vector as "just a bivector represented by its dual" ([Hestenes, Oersted lecture](https://davidhestenes.net/geocalc/pdf/OerstedMedalLecture.pdf)), and recent intro proposals teach angular velocity and magnetic field as oriented tiles with no hand rules ([Jensen and Poling, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03560); [Jensen, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02548)).

### Motion, words, and rounding each have a literature but no shared curriculum

Rapidity is as old as kraemer says. Varićak and Whittaker introduced it in 1910, Robb named it in 1911 ([Wikipedia, "Rapidity"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapidity)), and today it's routine at hadron colliders because rapidity differences don't change under boosts along the beam ([Wikipedia, "Pseudorapidity"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudorapidity)). Milne used the Doppler-factor method in 1935, and Bondi made it a lay book in 1964, calling k "a fundamental ratio"; rapidities add because k-factors multiply ([Wikipedia, "Bondi k-calculus"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bondi_k-calculus)). kraemer's shift factor s = e^w is Bondi's k. His stretch and half-gap, the even and odd parts (s + 1/s)/2 and (s − 1/s)/2, are γ and γv/c, so energy is mc² times the stretch and momentum is mc times the half-gap: the "motion, not velocity" piece lands exactly on four-momentum. Two older handles fit his operator theme. Lévy-Leblond read rapidity as what an ideal inertial-guidance system would report, and Wilson read it as the change in pitch dead ahead versus dead behind ([Wilson, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/0706.3247)); Wilson also called relativistic velocity addition a "pedagogical cul-de-sac" ([Wilson, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0611192)). On the momentum side, Newton's Law II is stated as change of "quantity of motion" ([Physics Hypertextbook](https://physics.info/newton-second/quote-newton.shtml)), and Matter & Interactions rebuilt intro mechanics on the momentum principle, iterated in VPython ([Kohlmyer et al., ASEE](https://peer.asee.org/3715.pdf)).

The language research diagnoses kraemer's problem thoroughly. Talmy showed that English force verbs (*keep*, *let*, *despite*) build in an object with an intrinsic tendency toward motion or rest, a model he calls "at considerable variance with modern physics," with a focus-versus-background asymmetry that Newton's third law doesn't have ([Talmy, ch. 7](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~talmy/talmyweb/Volume1/chap7.pdf)). The Force Concept Inventory's authors note that students use "force" for what's really impetus ([Hestenes et al. 1992](https://gwern.net/doc/science/physics/1992-hestenes.pdf)). In one vocabulary study, **59%** of pre-course sentences used "force" as a verb, "momentum" was easier than "impulse" because students "always related it to motion," and the everyday and physics meanings ended up coexisting rather than one replacing the other ([Itza-Ortiz et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0207066)). Brookes and Etkina showed that grammar sorts concepts into things, processes, and states that students take literally ("heat flows") ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0704.1319)), which bears directly on using capture and sweep as verbs. Sherin's symbolic forms (base ± change, parts of a whole, balancing) are the nearest research counterpart to "every sentence maps onto algebra" ([Sherin PERC slides](https://web.phys.ksu.edu/perc2007/formats/sherin-invited.pdf)). On rounding, Caltech's Ph 101 gives full credit for "a well-justified roughly correct answer" ([Ph 101](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~oom/description2025.html)), and Mahajan's books use g = 10 and π ≈ 3 and aim for "insight over accuracy" ([*Art of Insight*](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-011-the-art-of-insight-in-science-and-engineering-mastering-complexity-fall-2014/mitres_6-011_f14_textbook.pdf)). The outcome evidence for estimation-first teaching is practitioner experience, not controlled studies.

## Reformers hit institutional walls, not learning walls

The most instructive failure is geometric algebra. Hestenes's own account explains why it didn't spread: "I have not ventured to teach GA in undergraduate courses," textbook demand was "near zero," colleagues were "invariably dismissive as soon as they detected deviation from standard practice," students had to learn it alone because no other course used it, and a colleague who adopted his text was denied tenure ([Oersted lecture](https://davidhestenes.net/geocalc/pdf/OerstedMedalLecture.pdf)). None of that is evidence students can't learn it. The newer intro proposals report only anecdote, and one carries a pointed warning: teaching the bivector and vector languages side by side "seemed repetitive and confusing" ([Jensen and Poling](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03560)). The same Hestenes, meanwhile, co-built the reform that did spread. Modeling Instruction reached **more than 10,200 teachers in 49 states** by 2018, at least 10% of US physics teachers, with FCI gains about double traditional instruction ([APS Forum on Education](https://engage.aps.org/fed/resources/newsletters/spring-2019)). It changed classroom practice, kept standard notation, ran on teacher-led summer workshops, and had a cheap common test. Even it depended on teachers: its first workshop group gained "a sobering 4%" ([Wells et al. 1995](https://davidhestenes.net/modeling/R&E/ModelingMethod-Physics_1995.pdf)).

Rapidity's history in teaching is a quieter version of the same story. Bondi aimed at a general audience, d'Inverno and Woodhouse used the k-calculus in university texts, and Dray's hyperbolic-trig treatment came out of Oregon State's Paradigms program ([Dray](https://sites.science.oregonstate.edu/~tevian/geometry)), yet none of it reports classroom outcomes. The one documented retreat is thinly sourced: a single mailing-list post says Taylor and Wheeler's *Spacetime Physics* discussed the "velocity parameter" at length in 1966 and dropped it in 1992, and the second edition's table of contents is consistent with that ([PHYS-L](https://phys-l.org/archives/1999/09_1999/msg00031.html); [2nd-ed. contents](https://www.eftaylor.com/spacetimephysics/00_cover_toc.pdf)).

Matter & Interactions is the closest thing to an assembled kit: a few principles, an atomic model, and computation from week one. Its mechanics result is the cautionary one. In a 5,000-student comparison, FCI post-tests ran **71.3% for traditional courses and 59.3% for M&I** from matched pretests, and the authors tie the gap to how much time each course spent on FCI-style content (homework share 0.57 vs 0.26) rather than to the approach being harder ([Caballero et al., arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/abs/1106.1055)). In electromagnetism, across four universities and 2,000 students, M&I students did significantly better on the BEMA ([Kohlmyer et al., PRST-PER](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevSTPER.5.020105)). The honest reading is mixed: a principle-first course looks worse on an instrument built around traditional coverage and better on one that isn't.

The pattern across all of this is that a piece survives where one community owns the whole pipeline: colliders, the Navy's manuals and schools, motor makers' datasheets, Caltech's one course. It dies where the next course downstream uses something else. That's the wall kraemer will meet, and a book with its own notebooks and check scripts is a small pipeline he controls end to end.

## The real gap is the joints, plus four studies nobody has run

The genuine gap isn't any single piece. It's that no field had a reason to make the pieces agree with each other, because each one only needed its corner. GNSS doesn't care about hand rules, the Navy didn't care about rapidity, and metrologists arguing about the radian don't teach motors. A self-consistent kit has interactions none of the source fields ever tested: the stroke shows up in capture's unit (Wb per stroke), the hidden arrow sets capture's sign, "minus means the opposite of" and the hidden ← are two separate places a sign can enter, and rounding has to survive all of it.

Several open niches turned up repeatedly. No curriculum found chooses words whose everyday meaning already matches the physics, and none borrows operators' vocabulary; the literature prescribes awareness ("ask what do you mean?") rather than word choice, and Arons and Karplus come closest only by putting experience before names ([Kurki-Suonio on Arons](https://www.mv.helsinki.fi/home/kurkisuo/6.3.C/98-EJP-ARONS.pdf)). Clement's "anchoring examples," where intuition already agrees with the physics, are the closest method ([UMass SRRI](https://srri.umass.edu/preconceptions_in_mechanics)), and practitioner words are a natural place to look for anchors, though nobody has tested that. No study compares teaching angle in turns against radians. No controlled study compares electron-flow and conventional-current instruction. No classroom outcome study exists for rapidity or the k-calculus with beginners, or for bivectors with intro students. And no study tests whether an explanation system that maps onto algebra helps anyone detect faked understanding. Each of these is small and cheap, and any one of them would be a first.

The risks are specific. Kustusch found that negative charges were a major source of right-hand-rule errors, "with some students ignoring the sign and others double-counting it," and two students deliberately switched to a left hand ([Kustusch, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1507.02364)). That's the exact failure a hidden arrow invites, so q = e ← has to be applied exactly once, and the scripts should check that it was. The Jensen and Poling warning about running two languages side by side matters too, because students will meet conventional arrows in every diode symbol; the "backward shadow" reading is designed as a bridge rather than a second language, but that design is untested. The M&I result says to pick instruments matched to the kit's aims, or to include enough FCI-style practice that the standard test doesn't misread the course. Talmy's analysis says to vet verbs for built-in impetus; "overhauling," which names one body driving another, passes that test better than *keep* or *let*. And Modeling's 4% first workshop says the words alone won't carry it; how a teacher uses them matters as much.

## Chatbots ace the answers and still slip on the arrows

Use is now near universal but the surveys conflict. In the UK, **95%** of undergraduates used AI in December 2025, and **12%** put AI text directly into assessed work, up from 3% two years earlier ([HEPI 2026](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/reports/student-generative-ai-survey-2026/)). US figures disagree: **85%** of about 1,000 students in a 2025 survey ([eWeek on Inside Higher Ed](https://www.eweek.com/fr/news/student-voice-survey-2025/)) versus roughly 57% in a far larger fall 2024 survey ([Poets&Quants on EDUCAUSE](https://poetsandquantsforundergrads.com/news/students-demand-more-than-just-wi-fi-report-highlights-gaps-in-tech-integration-career-readiness/)), a gap that timing and wording plausibly explain but the summaries can't settle. Among US teens, ChatGPT use for schoolwork doubled from 13% to 26% between 2023 and 2024, and teens split evenly on whether using it to solve math problems is acceptable (29% vs 28%) ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/15/about-a-quarter-of-us-teens-have-used-chatgpt-for-schoolwork-double-the-share-in-2023)). No survey gives a usable physics breakdown.

Models went from barely passing to top of the class in about two years. ChatGPT in January 2023 "would narrowly pass" a calculus-based intro course "while exhibiting many of the preconceptions and errors of a beginning learner" ([Kortemeyer, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12127)). By 2025 GPT-5 Thinking scored **96% and 89%** on two semesters' finals ([Kortemeyer, *The Physics Teacher* 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.08842)), and an agent built on Gemini 2.5 Pro beat the gold-medalist median on the 2025 Physics Olympiad theory papers ([Qiu et al., arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01659v1)). The weak spots that remain are kraemer's territory. A 2026 Uppsala thesis found vision-language models fragile on graph interpretation, spatial coordination, and **right-hand-rule procedures**, stating plausible strategies while misreading the picture ([Polverini, Uppsala](https://www.uu.se/en/events/defences/2026-06-09-giulia-polverini-vision-language-model-performance-on-physics-tasks-exploring-ai-capabilities-on-research-based-assessments-involving-visual-representations)). A 2026 preprint on a new relativity inventory found correct final answers reached through flawed reasoning, with wrong models converging on a single distractor ([Tufino et al., arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09602)).

The strongest causal evidence of harm comes from math, not physics. In a preregistered field experiment with nearly 1,000 Turkish high school students, unguarded GPT-4 raised practice grades by 48% and then left students **17% worse** than controls on the unaided exam; a hint-only tutor avoided the harm but didn't beat the controls, and users overestimated how much they'd learned ([Bastani et al., PNAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html); [Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/)). A 2,379-student randomized university trial, still a working paper, found a course-integrated tutor that mostly gave direct answers lowered final grades by about 0.37 SD in the matched subsample, though only at the 10% significance level in the full sample ([Liu et al., IZA working paper](https://docs.iza.org/dp18989.pdf)). Smaller preprints point the same way: students who had a model generate solutions understood less than those who asked it for explanations ([Lehmann et al., arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047v2)), and in a 27-student physics study nearly half of ChatGPT-backed solutions were wrongly assumed correct ([Krupp et al., arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2309.03087)). In one intro physics course, unsupervised quiz scores tracked how students sought help while supervised exams didn't, which means the unsupervised numbers had stopped measuring understanding ([Kortemeyer and Bauer, arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2312.15334)).

The benefit evidence fits the harm evidence rather than contradicting it. Harvard's intro-physics crossover trial found an AI tutor beat in-class active learning with an effect of about 0.63 SD on immediate post-tests, but the system prompt alone "could not reliably scaffold multi-part problems," and its accuracy **relied on pre-written, step-by-step solutions** ([Kestin et al., Scientific Reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)). There's no retention measure, and no replication has appeared. A widely cited meta-analysis claiming a large benefit (g = 0.867) was retracted in May 2026 ([Plagiarism Today](https://www.plagiarismtoday.com/2026/07/15/study-claiming-ai-helps-students-learn-retracted/)), so don't lean on it. Physics leaders now recommend dropping unsupervised closed-response work for credit, grading process, using oral spot checks, and assigning diagnose-and-repair tasks ([Kortemeyer, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.08842)); no AAPT or APS teaching statement on generative AI turned up.

Taken together, the evidence supports a narrower version of kraemer's urgency claim, and it's still a strong one. A correct answer no longer signals understanding. The places where models and model-reliant students fail together (directions, diagrams, hand rules, multi-step sign bookkeeping) are where a step-by-step algebraic check would expose the slip. Every effective AI tutor or grader in this record was anchored to human-written, checkable solutions, which is the role his check scripts would play. And in one small preprint, the pre-service teachers who'd solved a problem themselves were the ones who checked an AI solution's units and signs ([Suárez and González, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.16830)). The honest limit is that models are now good at algebra too, so checkable algebra on take-home work doesn't stop faking by itself; it pays off where the check happens live, at a whiteboard or in an oral spot check, and there a kit whose sentences each map onto one line of algebra makes the check fast. One untested side effect may help: a model trained on textbook conventions will tend to drift back to radians, conventional current, and negative forces, so pasted output in a turns-and-arrows course would be easy to spot.

## Conclusion

What's new here isn't any piece; it's consistency as the product. Each source field fixed exactly what it needed and kept a private conversion at its edge, so the one thing no field ever had a reason to build is a check that the conversions agree. That's work check scripts do well and beginners can't do alone, and it's the part a chatbot can't fake for a student who has to defend it live. The survival record suggests kraemer's best protection is the one Hestenes lacked and the Navy had: control of the whole pipeline, from first sentence to the script that checks it, with a deliberate, single bridge to the conventions students will meet next.

The fastest way to turn the posited pieces into tested ones is the cheapest study on the list. A Kustusch-style set of hand-rule and charge-sign items, given to students taught with the hidden arrow and to students taught conventionally, would test the riskiest piece directly, since that's where both students and models already fail, and nobody has run it.
