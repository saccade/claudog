## Dining room
## 1. The knife
The loose sheet models the knife as a 1.0 kg point mass, a meter from its pivot. Let go from 0.10 rad off the downward vertical, it swings under g = 10 m/s². The numbers are on the sheet; the knife has already begun.
(1-a) Leaning through θ lifts a point ℓ away by ℓ(1 − cos θ). For this small lean, that’s ℓ θ²/2: 0.0050 m, or 5 mm. The 10 N weight lifted through 5 mm stores 0.050 J. Energy stored in height is heft: the weight pulls, and the height hefts. The knife hefts 0.050 J.
(1-b) At the bottom the heft has all gone into the knife’s motion. Energy in a body's motion is its punch, ½mv². The heft mgh and the punch ½mv² carry the same mass. Take it out: punch per kilogram is the zip, ½v², a quantity with the body's mass taken out, and heft per kilogram is gh. It cancels:
v² = 2gh.
A 5 mm fall under 10 m/s² hefts 0.050 J/kg, which all becomes zip: v² = 0.10 m²/s². The knife passes the bottom at 0.32 m/s.
(1-c) The knife has a cycle: from one end, through the bottom to the other end, and back. It cycles. Call the time for one complete cycle T.
Call a radian a stroke. All the way round is a turn, about 6.3 strokes, and half of that is π, the flip. A flip of the knife’s cycle takes it to the opposite end; a turn brings it back.
Count strokes each second and call that the sweep. The cycle’s sweep is ω. The usual small-swing rule balances as
ℓω² = g,
Tω = 2π.
Here the cycle sweeps at √10, about 3.2 strokes a second. Its cycle takes T = 2π/√10, about two seconds. There's a 2 riding along with that π: two flips standing in for one turn. π only covers half of what the knife does, one swing across; the cycle is the whole of it. Give the whole turn its own sign, τ, and the rule says so:
Tω = τ.
The knife’s own direction sweeps too, at a different pace. At the bottom it sweeps at v/ℓ, about 0.32 strokes a second. At either end that sweep is zero. The knife turns back while the cycle sweeps on. The rate the sweep changes is the pickup: swung θ off vertical, the knife picks up at g/ℓ of its lean, back toward the bottom, θ̈ = −(g/ℓ)θ. A stroke a second makes a handy unit of sweep, so call it a sweep too: the cycle sweeps at 3.2 sweep. At either end the knife picks up at 10 of its 0.10 lean: 1.0 sweep a second, back toward the bottom.
(1-d) Twice the mass doubles the heft, the punch, and the resistance to being sped up. The height, bottom speed, and time for a cycle stay the same. The cycle never asked for the mass.
The pen begins the curve again. The lid lifts.

(1-β) Each shape is a tone, and each tone's cycle sweeps at its own rate: ω_a² = (2 − √2)g/ℓ and ω_b² = (2 + √2)g/ℓ, whose product is 2(g/ℓ)². (1-γ) Both tones at once make a chord. Their relative phase sweeps at the gap, ω_b − ω_a = 3.42 sweep. ## 2. The engine and the flywheel
(2-a) The engine ends each cycle with the same stored energy it began with. Of the 15 J that comes in (heat-in), 3.0 J comes out as work and the other 12 J leaves (heat-out). Work over heat-in is 3/15: 20%. At a cycle a second, that's 3.0 W.
(2-b) Energy in turning is hook: a flywheel hooks half its moment of inertia at the squared sweep. 0.50 kg m² sweeping at 4.0 sweep hooks 4.0 J. Four seconds at a net 3.0 W add 12 J of hook, for 16 J, and 16 J of hook on the same wheel needs the squared sweep to be 64. The wheel ends up sweeping at 8.0. The engine paid in equal hook every second, not equal sweep, so four times the hook only doubles the sweep. With the inertia taken out, hook is the zing, ½ω²: the wheel zings at 8 at the start and 32 at the end. Power on the inertia is the zlow, and its verb is zlower: 3.0 W on 0.50 kg m² is a zlow of 6, so the engine zlowers the wheel at 6 zing a second, steadily, 8 to 32 in four seconds. Power on the mass would be the zow, and its verb zower.
(2-c) Torque usually gets the letter τ, but the turn has it now. A χ hung on the wall could apply a twist to something—use that for torque. A torque cranks. The power a crank delivers is the sweep of the crank: P = ωχ. Put numbers to it once: 0.75 N m cranking at 4.0 sweep delivers 3.0 W. So at the start the engine cranks the wheel with χ = 0.75 N m, and at the end, sweeping at 8.0, with 0.38 N m.
When the wheel goes twice as fast, the same power cranks just half as hard. On 0.50 kg m², those cranks pick the wheel up at 1.5 sweep a second at the start and 0.75 at the end.
## 3. The governor and the spool

(3-c) With the mass taken out, inertia is the profile: I on the spool's 2.0 kg is a profile of 0.020 m² about the axle. The cord pulls forward (→), and the counter's grip f is taken → too; the answer will say which way it really goes. The center of mass speeds up as if both forces acted on it directly: ma → = F → + f →. Rolling →, the spool turns back around, ↻: the turning carries the bottom back, ↻ × ↓ = ←. Both pulls act below the axle, the cord 0.12 m and the grip 0.20 m, and ↓ × → = ↺, so the cord cranks the spool ↺ about the axle with Fr and the grip cranks it ↺ with fR: Iα ↻ = [Fr + fR] ↺. The two sides carry opposite senses (↻ = −↺), and the numbers say which way it really goes. Rolling ties the pickup to the center: α = a/R. Put together, the ledgers balance as:
(m + I/R²)a = F(1 − r/R).
The profile over R² is 0.020/0.040 = 1/2, so the mass and the turning count as 2.0 kg at 3/2: 3.0 kg at the axle, while 6.0 N × 0.40 gives 2.4 N on the other side. The center speeds up at 0.80 m/s² →: the spool rolls toward the pull, picking up at 4.0 sweep a second. The grip is what's left when the cord's 6.0 N → comes off the 1.6 N → the center needs: f = 1.6 N → + 6.0 N ← = 4.4 N ←. It pulls back, and about the axle it cranks the spool ↻ harder than the cord cranks it ↺.
(3-d) A spool that stays put has a = 0 and α = 0. The cord still leaves the hub at a tangent, 0.12 m from the axle, so it still cranks the spool ↺ with Fr; only its forward part changes, to → · F, the F cos θ along the counter. With nothing speeding up, the grip is the same size pointing back, → · F · ←, which read from the other end is ← · F · →: pulling 0.20 m below the axle, it cranks the spool ↺ along with the cord, by (← · F) R. The two turnings add, Fr ↺ + (← · F) R ↺, and with nothing turning that's zero. So the forward part is r/R of the pull, and cos θ = 0.60. That's θ = 53°: the 3–4–5 again, with the 3 along the counter. The spool's inertia never comes into it, because a spool that doesn't pick up never asks for it.

## 9. The double pendulum
(9-a) Each mode cycles with its own sweep, and a turn of it takes T = τ/ω. The slow one takes τ/2.42 = 2.60 s and the fast one τ/5.84 = 1.08 s.
(9-b) The squares are in the ratio (2 + √2)/(2 − √2). Multiply top and bottom by 2 + √2 and it's (2 + √2)²/2, so the sweeps are in the ratio (2 + √2)/√2 = 1 + √2. Flip it and the slow over the fast is √2 − 1: the two multiply to one. That's irrational, so no whole number of slow cycles ever equals a whole number of fast ones, and a motion with both in it never exactly repeats.
(9-c) Each mode is a tone, and its shape is how far the lower rod leans for the upper's lean: Ω_shape = +√2 for the slow tone, both masses swinging the same way, the lower one farther; and −√2 for the fast tone, the lower rod flipped.
The shape is the rods'. Each mass is out by its own rod's length of its own swing, and the lower one rides on the upper: the L of the U has nothing to be. With the body written last, swinging the upper rod 0.10 rad → puts them at 0.10 m → × U + 0.24 m → × L in the slow tone and 0.10 m → × U + 0.041 m ← × L in the fast: the lower mass goes the other way, but only √2 − 1 as far.
In either tone, each rod picks up at ω² of its own lean, back toward vertical. Both tones at once make a chord, and the chord beats at the gap, 5.84 − 2.42 = 3.42 sweep.
## 10. The coils and the capacitor
(10-a) Say these the right way round. Current is the conductance of the voltage: I = GV, with G = 1/R. Power is the voltage of the current: P = VI. A capacitor is a can: it cans charge, and the charge it cans is the capacitance of its voltage, q = CV. At the start the can holds no charge, so the resistor gets the whole 6.0 V, and the current is the conductance of 6.0 V across 20 Ω: 0.30 A. The time constant is RC = 20 × 0.10 = 2.0 s. When the can's at 4.0 V, the resistor has the other 2.0 V, and the current is 0.10 A. The can holds 0.40 C and ½CV² = 0.80 J, and the resistor's burning the voltage of the current, 2.0 V of 0.10 A, 0.20 W, as heat.
This is a different can, in a separate loop with a coil. A coil coils flux, and the flux it coils is the inductance of its current, Φ = Li.
(10-b) With C = 1/(Lω_a²), LC = 1/ω_a², so ω₀ = 1/√(LC) = ω_a = 2.42 sweep. The loop on its own cycles with the slow mode exactly.
(10-c) The statement writes the torque τ_em; here it's χ_em = κi: the current cranks the rod. The power is the rod's sweep of that crank, κiȧ. The statement's minus in ε = −κȧ is a direction: the rod's motion drives an emf of κȧ back against the current that cranks it, so the circuit gives up the voltage of the current, κȧ of i: the same κiȧ. Whatever the rod gets, the circuit gives up. When the current cranks the rod against its sweep, the energy runs the other way, and the rod's giving its energy to the circuit instead. It's a trade: a belt, not a battery. Nothing's made.
(10-d) (2/3) × 2.42 = 1.61 and (3/2) × 2.42 = 3.63 sweep. Their product is ω₀², 5.86 per second squared, and the faster is 9/4 of the slower: 4 to 9. They're a mirror pair about ω₀: 2/3 and its flip, 3/2. These are the two tones of one machine: rods and loop together.

## Mirror 2: the trade, both ways

(2-α) Put the trial motions in. The swing's balance gives (D − ηω²)A = κωQ and the loop's gives (1/C − Lω²)Q = κωA. Multiply them; D = ηω₀² and 1/C = Lω₀² turn the left side into ηL(ω₀² − ω²)²AQ, so ηL(ω₀² − ω²)² = κ²ω². The charge runs a quarter turn of the cycle from the lean.

## 11. The line
(11-a) Per meter, the line cans C′ and coils L′. Its values balance as L′C′u² = 1 and C′Z² = L′. Here L′C′ = 10⁻¹⁶ s²/m², so u = 1.0 × 10⁸ m/s, a third of light’s speed. The impedance balance gives Z² = 2500 ohm², so Z = 50 ohm.
(11-b) The voltages add: 12 V. Each pulse's current is 6.0/50 = 0.12 A in its own direction of travel, and they travel opposite ways, so the currents cancel: no current at all. The energy per meter is all canned there, none coiled: ½ × 0.20 × 10⁻⁹ × 144 = 14 nJ/m. That's the same as the two pulses' 7.2 nJ/m each, so nothing's gone missing; it's just all in one place.
(11-c) The sum is 9.0 MHz and the difference 1.0 MHz, so the crests move at u/9 = 1.1 × 10⁷ m/s and at 9u = 9.0 × 10⁸ m/s, three times light's speed. The fast crests can't carry a signal. A signal is a change made at a source, and the change travels with its wave at u. The fast crest is only where two waves that were already there happen to line up. It seems to arrive before anyone sends anything because nobody sent it. The fast crest is a stripe: a ghost body with a place, a velocity, and a phase, and nothing stored. Phase is for ghosts. The two crest speeds are each other's flip about u: u/9 and 9u multiply to u².

(13-a) The can holds q₀²/(2C), with C = 1/(2 − √2) F, about 1.71 F. Setting that to 0.080 J gives q₀ = √(2 × 1.71 × 0.080) = 0.52 C, and the voltage is q₀/C = 0.31 V.
(13-b) The two cosine weights are positive and add to one, so q reaches its initial maximum q₀ only when both cosines return to 1. ## β: TEST, then RUN
On the chassis: a switch with two positions, TEST and RUN, and a small drive arm linked to the mechanical coordinate a.
(β-1) Put ω₀X and Ẋ along the two directions of a state arrow. Its squared length is twice the oscillator’s energy. Between taps the arrow turns with the cycle; each tap adds the same piece along Ẋ. One whole turn between taps brings every addition back into step, so N additions make the arrow N times as long and store N²E₁. The k-th adds k² − (k − 1)² = 2k − 1 units of work. Equal impulses don’t mean equal work: the drive pays more as the motion grows.
(β-2) Landing at random points, the arrows point every which way. The square of the sum is the sum of the squares plus cross terms, and the cross terms average to zero, so the expected energy is NE₁: a random walk.
(β-3) A hundred taps in step give a hundred times one tap’s amplitude and 10,000 E₁. Random phases give an expected 100 E₁ and a root-mean-square amplitude ten times one tap’s. The coherent case stores a hundred times the expected energy. The drive supplies the work in either case.
(β-4) No. In RUN the drive is disconnected and the loop is closed. The machine keeps its 80 mJ; the return puts it all back in the capacitor. The build-up in TEST came from the drive, through its repeated work. Changing the timing can break that step; simply making two free tones line up adds nothing.
