## Dining room
## 1. The knife
A knife is modeled as a point mass m = 1.0 kg on a massless rod of length ℓ = 1.0 m, pivoted to swing freely in a vertical plane. It gets released from rest at θ₀ = 0.10 rad from the downward vertical. Use g = 10 m/s² and the small-angle approximation 1 − cos θ ≈ θ²/2.
1. Find the gravitational potential energy of the knife at release, relative to its lowest point.
2. Find its greatest speed.
3. For small oscillations the angular frequency is ω₀ = √(g/ℓ), and the period T satisfies ω₀T = 2π. Find T.
4. How does T change if the mass is doubled?
Solution
The loose sheet models the knife as a 1.0 kg point mass, a meter from its pivot. Let go from 0.10 rad off the downward vertical, it swings under g = 10 m/s². The numbers are on the sheet; the knife has already begun.
(1) Leaning through θ lifts a point ℓ away by ℓ(1 − cos θ). For this small lean, that’s ℓ θ²/2: 0.0050 m, or 5 mm. The 10 N weight lifted through 5 mm stores 0.050 J. Energy stored in height is heft: the weight pulls, and the height hefts. The knife hefts 0.050 J.
(2) At the bottom the heft has all gone into the knife’s motion. Energy in a body's motion is its punch, ½mv². The heft mgh and the punch ½mv² carry the same mass. Take it out: punch per kilogram is the zip, ½v², a quantity with the body's mass taken out, and heft per kilogram is gh. It cancels:
v² = 2gh.
A 5 mm fall under 10 m/s² hefts 0.050 J/kg, which all becomes zip: v² = 0.10 m²/s². The knife passes the bottom at 0.32 m/s.
(3) The knife has a cycle: from one end, through the bottom to the other end, and back. It cycles. Call the time for one complete cycle T.
Call a radian a stroke. All the way round is a turn, about 6.3 strokes, and half of that is π, the flip. A flip of the knife’s cycle takes it to the opposite end; a turn brings it back.
Count strokes each second and call that the sweep. The cycle’s sweep is ω. The usual small-swing rule balances as
ℓω² = g,
Tω = 2π.
Here the cycle sweeps at √10, about 3.2 strokes a second. Its cycle takes T = 2π/√10 = 1.99 s, about two seconds. There's a 2 riding along with that π: two flips standing in for one turn. π only covers half of what the knife does, one swing across; the cycle is the whole of it. Give the whole turn its own sign, τ, and the rule says so:
Tω = τ.
The knife’s own direction sweeps too, at a different pace. At the bottom it sweeps at v/ℓ, about 0.32 strokes a second. At either end that sweep is zero. The knife turns back while the cycle sweeps on. The rate the sweep changes is the pickup: swung θ off vertical, the knife picks up at g/ℓ of its lean, back toward the bottom, θ̈ = −(g/ℓ)θ. A stroke a second makes a handy unit of sweep, so call it a sweep too: the cycle sweeps at 3.2 sweep. At either end the knife picks up at 10 of its 0.10 lean: 1.0 sweep a second, back toward the bottom.
(4) Twice the mass doubles the heft, the punch and the resistance to being sped up. The height, bottom speed and time for a cycle stay the same. The cycle never asked for the mass.
The pen begins the curve again. The lid lifts.

## Drawer 4: the second knife's two tones
Door: the dining room. A silver canteen with an enamel juggler on the lid, lined in damask; inside, under glass, one knife hanging from another.
Two point masses m hang in series on light rods each ℓ long, swinging in one vertical plane. For small angles θ₁ (upper rod) and θ₂ (lower rod) from the downward vertical, positive toward the same side, 2θ̈₁ + θ̈₂ = −2(g/ℓ)θ₁ and θ̈₁ + θ̈₂ = −(g/ℓ)θ₂.
1. In a normal mode both angles go as cos ωt, with θ₂ = +√2 θ₁ or θ₂ = −√2 θ₁. Find ω² for each.
2. With ℓ = 1.0 m and g = 10 m/s², find both ω and their ratio. Does a motion with both modes ever exactly repeat?
Solution
(1) In a mode both rods pick up at ω² of their angle, back toward vertical. The first balance gives (2 ± √2)ω²θ₁ = 2(g/ℓ)θ₁, so ω² = 2g/((2 ± √2)ℓ) = (2 ∓ √2)g/ℓ. The second balance gives the same two. The shape where the lower rod leans the same way, +√2, is the slow one.
(2) ω² = 10(2 − √2) = 5.86 and 10(2 + √2) = 34.1 per second squared, so the slow mode cycles at 2.42 sweep and the fast one at 5.84. Their ratio is (2 + √2)/√2 = 1 + √2. That's irrational, so a motion with both modes never quite repeats.

## F1: the knives, flipped
A long mirror in which the second knife hangs upside down.
The double pendulum of drawer 4 has 2θ̈₁ + θ̈₂ = −2(g/ℓ)θ₁ and θ̈₁ + θ̈₂ = −(g/ℓ)θ₂, with ℓ = 1.0 m and g = 10 m/s².
1. In a normal mode θ₂ = rθ₁ and both angles go as cos ωt. Use both equations to show that r² = 2.
2. Show that the two modes' angular frequencies satisfy ω_b/ω_a = 1 + √2 and ω_a/ω_b = √2 − 1, and that ω_a²ω_b² = 2(g/ℓ)².
3. At what angular rate does the relative phase of the two modes advance? For equal contributions to θ₁ that begin in phase, how long is the interval between successive reinforcements? Does that interval return the whole state?
Solution
(1) In a mode both rods pick up at ω² of their angle, back toward vertical, and the lower rod leans Ω_shape of the upper's lean. The first balance gives (2 + Ω_shape)ω² = 2g/ℓ and the second (1 + Ω_shape)ω² = Ω_shape g/ℓ. Divide one by the other: (2 + Ω_shape)Ω_shape = 2(1 + Ω_shape), so Ω_shape² = 2. The shape is +√2 or −√2: the same lean, or the lower rod flipped.
(2) Each shape is a tone, and each tone's cycle sweeps at its own rate: ω_a² = (2 − √2)g/ℓ and ω_b² = (2 + √2)g/ℓ, whose product is 2(g/ℓ)². The fast over the slow is √((2 + √2)/(2 − √2)) = 1 + √2, and the slow over the fast is its reflection, √2 − 1. The two multiply to one. Flip the lower rod and you've flipped the tone.
(3) Both tones at once make a chord. Their relative phase sweeps at the gap, ω_b − ω_a = 3.42 sweep. Equal contributions to the upper lean reinforce once per turn of that difference: 1.84 s between reinforcements. The signed cosine envelope takes twice that time to return; its sign flips between neighboring reinforcements. Neither interval returns the whole chord. The two tones’ ratio is irrational.

## 2. The engine and the flywheel
A small steam engine takes in 15 J of heat and does 3.0 J of work in each cycle, returning to its initial state at the end of each cycle. It completes one cycle per second.
1. Find the heat rejected per cycle, the efficiency and the average power output.
2. The engine's 3.0 W of net power, taken as constant, drives a flywheel of moment of inertia I = 0.50 kg m², initially turning at 4.0 rad/s, for 4.0 s. Find the final angular speed.
3. The power delivered by a torque τ to a wheel turning at angular speed ω is P = τω. Find the driving torque at the start and at the end of the 4.0 s.
Solution
(1) The engine ends each cycle with the same stored energy it began with. Of 15 J of heat-in, 3.0 J comes out as work and the other 12 J is heat-out. Work over heat-in is 3/15: 20%. At a cycle a second, that's 3.0 W.
(2) Energy in turning is hook: a flywheel hooks half its moment of inertia at the squared sweep. 0.50 kg m² sweeping at 4.0 sweep hooks 4.0 J. Four seconds at a net 3.0 W add 12 J of hook, for 16 J, and 16 J of hook on the same wheel needs the squared sweep to be 64. The wheel ends up sweeping at 8.0. The engine paid in equal hook every second, not equal sweep, so four times the hook only doubles the sweep. With the inertia taken out, hook is the zing, ½ω²: the wheel zings at 8 at the start and 32 at the end. Power on the inertia is the zlow, and its verb is zlower: 3.0 W on 0.50 kg m² is a zlow of 6, so the engine zlowers the wheel at 6 zing a second, steadily, 8 to 32 in four seconds. Power on the mass would be the zow, and its verb zower.
(3) Torque usually gets the letter τ, but the turn has it now. A χ hung on the wall could apply a twist to something—use that for torque. A torque cranks. The power a crank delivers is the sweep of the crank: P = ωχ. Put numbers to it once: 0.75 N m cranking at 4.0 sweep delivers 3.0 W. So at the start the engine cranks the wheel with χ = 0.75 N m, and at the end, sweeping at 8.0, with 0.38 N m.
When the wheel goes twice as fast, the same power cranks just half as hard. On 0.50 kg m², those cranks pick the wheel up at 1.5 sweep a second at the start and 0.75 at the end.
## 3. The governor and the spool
A centrifugal governor has two 1.0 kg balls, each on a massless 0.5 m arm hinged on the axis of a vertical shaft. The shaft turns steadily at 5.0 rad/s†.
1. Find the angle each arm makes with the vertical, and how far each ball has risen above its hanging position.
2. Below what angular speed would the arms hang straight down?
3. A spool rests on a level counter. It has mass 2.0 kg, outer radius R = 0.2 m, hub radius r = 0.12 m, and moment of inertia 0.04 kg m²† about its axle. A light cord wound on the hub leaves from the bottom of the hub and is pulled horizontally with a constant 6.0 N. If the spool rolls without slipping, how does it accelerate?
4. If the cord is instead pulled partly upward, at what angle does the spool stay at rest?
Solution
(1) The arm's pull does two jobs at once. Its upward part holds up the ball's weight, mg. Its inward part bends the ball's path round the shaft, and that takes the mass at the squared sweep, at the ball's distance from the shaft: mω²ℓ sin θ. The pull runs along the arm, so the two parts stand in the arm's own slope: tan θ = ℓω² sin θ/g. For an arm off the vertical the sine cancels, and cos θ = g/(ℓω²) = 10/(0.50 × 25) = 0.80. The arm hangs 0.40 m down and reaches 0.30 m out: a 3–4–5 triangle a tenth of a meter to the unit, about 37° from the vertical. Each ball hangs 0.40 m below the hinge instead of 0.50 m, so it's risen 0.10 m, and each 1.0 kg ball hefts 1.0 J.
(2) The ball hangs g/ω² below the hinge: 10/25 = 0.40 m here. A drop can't be longer than the arm. Below a sweep of √(g/ℓ) = √20, about 4.5 sweep, g/ω² would be longer than the 0.50 m arm, so hanging straight down is all that's left.
(3) With the mass taken out, inertia is the profile: I on the spool's 2.0 kg is a profile of 0.020 m² about the axle. The cord pulls forward (→). Take the counter's grip on the spool, f, as positive → too; the answer will say which way it really goes. The center of mass speeds up as if both forces acted on it directly: ma = F + f. Rolling →, the spool turns back around, ↻. The cord pulls → 0.12 m below the axle and the grip acts 0.20 m below it, and a → push below the axle turns the spool forward around, ↺. So the cord cranks the spool ↺ about the axle with Fr, the grip cranks it ↺ with fR, and Iα ↻ = [Fr + fR] ↺. Rolling ties the pickup to the center: α = a/R. Put together, the ledgers balance as:
(m + I/R²)a = F(1 − r/R).
The profile over R² is 0.020/0.040 = 1/2, so the mass and the turning count as 2.0 kg at 3/2: 3.0 kg at the axle, while 6.0 N × 0.40 gives 2.4 N on the other side. The center speeds up at 0.80 m/s² →: the spool rolls toward the pull, picking up at 4.0 sweep a second. The grip is what's left when the cord's 6.0 N → comes off the 1.6 N → the center needs: f = 1.6 N → + 6.0 N ← = 4.4 N ←. It pulls back, and about the axle it cranks the spool ↻ harder than the cord cranks it ↺.
(4) A spool that stays put has a = 0 and α = 0. The cord still leaves the hub at a tangent, 0.12 m from the axle, so it still cranks the spool ↺ with Fr; only its forward part changes, to F cos θ →. With nothing speeding up, the grip has to be F cos θ ←, and pulling ← 0.20 m below the axle it cranks the spool ↻ with F cos θ at R. The two balance when F cos θ at R is Fr, so cos θ = r/R = 0.60. That's θ = 53°: the 3–4–5 again, with the 3 along the counter. The spool's inertia never comes into it, because a spool that doesn't pick up never asks for it.
## Drawer 1: the kitchen's two 3–4–5s
Door: the kitchen. A toolbox in scorched canvas, patched with the same cloth turned inside out, its lid painted with a ringmaster's star; inside, a little governor on a stand and a spool on a strip of counter.
Use g = 10 m/s².
1. A ball hangs on a light arm of length ℓ = 0.50 m, hinged on the axis of a vertical shaft that turns steadily at angular speed ω. Show that the arm settles at an angle θ from the vertical with cos θ = g/(ℓω²).
2. Find the ω at which the arm's vertical drop and horizontal reach are in the ratio 4 to 3, and how far the ball has risen from hanging straight down.
3. A spool of mass m, outer radius R, hub radius r and moment of inertia I about its axle rests on a level counter. A cord wound on the hub leaves from the bottom of the hub and is pulled horizontally with force F. The spool rolls without slipping. Show that its center's acceleration is a = F(1 − r/R)/(m + I/R²).
4. For m = 2.0 kg and R = 0.20 m, find the I that makes m + I/R² equal 3.0 kg. What simple shape of spool has that I? With r = 0.12 m and F = 6.0 N, find a and the friction force.
5. The cord is instead pulled at an angle θ above the horizontal. Show that the spool can stay at rest only when cos θ = r/R, whatever I is, and find θ.
Solution
(1) The arm's pull on the ball does two jobs at once. Its upward part holds the weight, mg. Its inward part bends the ball's path round the axis, mℓ sin θ ω². The pull runs along the arm, so the two jobs stand in the arm's own slope: tan θ = ℓ sin θ ω²/g. For an arm off the vertical the sine cancels, and cos θ = g/(ℓω²). Hanging straight down is the other answer, and below ω = √(g/ℓ) it's the only one.
(2) A drop of 4 to a reach of 3 makes the arm the 5, so cos θ = 4/5. Then ω² = g/(ℓ · 4/5) = 10/0.40 = 25, and ω = 5.0 sweep: the kitchen's shaft. The ball hangs 0.40 m below the hinge instead of 0.50 m, so it’s risen 0.10 m.
(3) Count about the line where the spool touches the counter. Friction and the counter's push act there, with no leverage about it. The contact's acceleration points straight at the axle, where the center of mass is, so counting about it is fair. The cord's line runs R − r above the counter and cranks the spool F(R − r) about the contact. The spool's profile about its center is I/m; about the contact it gains R², so its inertia there is I + mR². The pickup is F(R − r) on I + mR², and the center speeds up at R of that:
a = F(1 − r/R)/(m + I/R²).
The center's ledgers agree. Take the counter's grip f as positive → too, like the cord: ma → = F → + f →. Rolling →, the spool turns back around, and both pulls below the axle crank it forward around: I a/R ↻ = [Fr + fR] ↺. Put the second into the first and the same a comes out. The grip comes out ←, so the cord cranks the spool ↺ about the axle and the counter's grip cranks it ↻, harder.
(4) The bottom line counts what must speed up at the axle: the mass, plus the turning carried as inertia over R². For 3.0 kg, I/R² = 1.0 kg, so I = 0.040 kg m², half of mR². That's a uniform disk. Then 6.0 N at 1 − 0.60 is 2.4 N on 3.0 kg: a = 0.80 m/s² →, the way the cord pulls. Friction pulls 4.4 N ←.
(5) A spool that stays put isn't turning, so the pull has no leverage about the contact. The cord leaves the hub at a tangent, r from the axle; tilted up θ, its line passes R cos θ − r from the contact. No leverage means cos θ = r/R. The inertia never enters, because a spool that doesn't turn never asks for it. With r/R = 0.60 = 3/5, θ = 53°: the 3–4–5 again, with the 3 along the counter.

## 4. The ladder
A uniform ladder of mass 8.0 kg and length 3.0 m rests with its foot 1.8 m from a frictionless wall and its top 2.4 m above a frictionless floor. Its moment of inertia about its center is mL²/12. Use g = 10 m/s².
1. The foot is pulled away from the wall at 0.40 m/s while the top stays against the wall. Find the speed of the top at that instant.
2. The ladder is released from rest in its original position. It stays in contact with the wall until its top is 1.60 m† above the floor. Find the gravitational potential energy released by then.
3. While both ends touch, the center moves at speed L ω/2, where ω is the ladder's angular speed. Find how that energy divides between the center's translation and the rotation about the center, and the speed of the center when the top leaves the wall.
Solution
(1) The ladder doesn't stretch: with the foot x out from the wall and the top y up, x² + y² = L² the whole time. As both change, x of the foot's velocity and y of the top's velocity cancel: 1.8 m of 0.40 m/s against 2.4 m of the top's. So the top moves at x/y of the foot's speed, the other way: 0.30 m/s, downward.
(2) For the release from rest, the center of mass starts at half of 2.4 m and ends at half of 1.60 m, so it drops 0.40 m. With 80 N of weight, that's 32 J of heft let go.
(3) The center runs at L ω/2 and the ladder sweeps about its center at the same ω. The center punches ½ m (L ω/2)², or m L² ω²/8. About the center the profile is L²/12, so the turning hooks ½ (m L²/12) ω², or m L² ω²/24. Those are 3 to 1, so the 32 J of heft splits 24 J of punch and 8 J of hook. Then 8.0 kg punching 24 J runs at √6, about 2.4 m/s, when the top leaves the wall. Or count about the point that's still for the instant, where the perpendiculars to the foot's motion and the top's meet: call it the still point. It's L/2 from the center, the corner turned a flip about the center, so the profile there is L²/12 + L²/4 = L²/3, and all 32 J is turning about it. Per kilogram, that's 3 J/kg of zip for the center and 1 for the turning. The ladder is sweeping at 2v/L, about 1.6 sweep.
## Drawer 2: the wall lets go
Door: the library. A gentleman's reading kit in circus colors, its velvet worn to the nap: a folding rule, spectacles, a little ladder leaning on a painted wall.
A uniform ladder of mass m and length L leans on a frictionless wall from a frictionless floor, its top at height h₀. It's released from rest. Use g = 10 m/s².
1. While both ends touch, show that the ladder's center stays L/2 from the corner where the wall meets the floor.
2. While both ends touch, show that the ladder's kinetic energy is (1/6)mL²ω², where ω is its angular speed.
3. The wall can push but can't pull. Show that the top leaves the wall when its height is 2h₀/3.
4. For L = 3.0 m and h₀ = 2.4 m, find that height and the center's speed then.
Solution
(1) Call the ladder's angle with the floor φ. The foot is L cos φ out and the top L sin φ up, so the center sits at half of each. That's a circle of radius L/2 about the corner.
(2) The center runs round its circle at (L/2)ω and punches ½m(L/2)²ω². The ladder's own turning hooks ½m(L²/12)ω² on top. The profiles add, L²/4 + L²/12 = L²/3, so the whole is ½m(L²/3)ω² = (1/6)mL²ω².
(3) With the top at height y, the center has dropped (h₀ − y)/2, so the heft let go is mg(h₀ − y)/2, and ω² = 3g(h₀ − y)/L². The wall's push is the only sideways force, so it's what speeds the center up sideways. The center's sideways speed is (L/2)ω sin φ = ωy/2, and its square, 3gy²(h₀ − y)/(4L²), grows while y²(h₀ − y) grows. That peaks where 2h₀y = 3y², at y = 2h₀/3. Past there the center would have to slow down sideways, and that takes a pull. The wall can't pull, so it lets go.
(4) Two thirds of 2.4 m is 1.6 m. Then ω² = 3 × 10 × 0.8/9 = 8/3, and the center runs at 1.5√(8/3) = √6 = 2.4 m/s.

## 5. The cue ball
A uniform solid ball of mass 1.0 kg and radius R = 0.050 m (moment of inertia 2 m R²/5) is at rest on a table. A cue gives it a short horizontal impulse of 2.0 N s at height H above the table. Neglect friction during the impulse.
1. Find the speed of the ball's center just afterward.
2. Find H if the ball rolls without slipping immediately.
3. Find its angular speed and its translational and rotational kinetic energies.
Solution
(1) Call the given impulse J: 2.0 N s →. The strike is a blow; what it leaves in the center's motion is punch. On 1.0 kg it leaves the center running at 2.0 m/s →.
(2) Take the lever from the ball's center up to where the cue strikes: (H − R) ↑. The angular momentum a blow leaves about the center is spin, and it's the leverage of the blow, with ↑ × → = ↻:
(H − R) ↑ × J → = (H − R) J ↻.
Rolling at once means the bottom of the ball isn't sliding. The bottom's lever from the center is R ↓, and the turning moves it at the sweep crossed into that lever: ω ↻ × R ↓ = ω R ←. That has to cancel the center's v →, so the ball sweeps at v/R ↻. Its profile about the center is (2/5) R², and (2/5) m R² sweeping at v/R carries
(2/5) m R² (v/R) ↻ = (2/5) R J ↻
of spin, since m v is J. The two senses agree, so the cue went in above the center, and H − R = 2R/5: H = 7R/5 = 0.070 m, two fifths of a radius above the center.
(3) The ball sweeps at 2.0/0.050 = 40 sweep ↻, and its spin is (2/5)(0.050 m)(2.0 N s) = 0.040 kg m²/s ↻. The center punches half its velocity dotted into its momentum, and the turning hooks half the sweep dotted into the spin:
½ (2.0 m/s →) · (2.0 N s →) = 2.0 J,
½ (40 ↻) · (0.040 kg m²/s ↻) = 0.80 J.

## 6. The basin
A small uniform solid ball rolls without slipping back and forth across the bottom of a fixed curved basin. Its center moves on a circular arc of radius a = 5/7 m†. For small oscillations its period is T = 2π√(7a/(5g)). Use g = 10 m/s².

## 7. The hoop

## 9. The double pendulum
Two point masses of 1.0 kg hang in series on massless rods each 1.0 m long, swinging in one vertical plane. Use g = 10 m/s². Its small oscillations are combinations of two normal modes†: a slow mode with ω_a² = 10(2 − √2) s⁻² (ω_a ≈ 2.42 rad/s), in which the lower rod's angle is +√2 times the upper rod's, and a fast mode with ω_b² = 10(2 + √2) s⁻² (ω_b ≈ 5.84 rad/s), in which it's −√2 times. Both angles are measured from the downward vertical, positive toward the same side.
1. Find the period of each mode.
2. Show that ω_b/ω_a = 1 + √2. Does a motion mixing both modes ever exactly repeat?
3. Describe each mode in words: which way does the lower mass move compared with the upper?
Solution
(1) Each mode cycles with its own sweep, and a turn of it takes T = τ/ω. The slow one takes τ/2.42 = 2.60 s and the fast one τ/5.84 = 1.08 s.
(2) The squares are in the ratio (2 + √2)/(2 − √2). Multiply top and bottom by 2 + √2 and it's (2 + √2)²/2, so the sweeps are in the ratio (2 + √2)/√2 = 1 + √2. Flip it and the slow over the fast is √2 − 1: the two multiply to one. That's irrational, so no whole number of slow cycles ever equals a whole number of fast ones, and a motion with both in it never exactly repeats.
(3) Each mode is a tone, and its shape is how far the lower rod leans for the upper's lean: Ω_shape = +√2 for the slow tone, both masses swinging the same way, the lower one farther; and −√2 for the fast tone, the lower rod flipped. In either tone, each rod picks up at ω² of its own lean, back toward vertical. Both tones at once make a chord, and the chord beats at the gap, 5.84 − 2.42 = 3.42 sweep.
## 10. The coils and the capacitor
1. A 6.0 V source is connected through a 20 Ω resistor to an uncharged 0.10 F capacitor. Find the initial current, the time constant, and the current, charge and stored energy when the capacitor reaches 4.0 V.
2. An ideal LC circuit has L = 0.10 H and C = 1/(Lω_a²) ≈ 1.71 F†, with ω_a from problem 9. Find its angular frequency ω₀ = 1/√(LC), and compare it with ω_a.
3. A coil fixed to a pendulum rod sits in a magnetic field. With the rod's angle a and the current i both signed, the current exerts a torque τ_em = κi on the rod, and the rod's motion induces an emf ε = −κȧ in the circuit. Show that the mechanical power delivered to the rod, κiȧ, equals the electrical power the circuit gives up, and that the exchange runs the other way when κi and ȧ have opposite signs.
4. When the slow pendulum mode of problem 9 and the LC circuit are coupled in this way, the combined system has two modes†, at (2/3)ω₀ and (3/2)ω₀. Find them, their product, and the ratio of their angular frequencies.
Solution
(1) Say these the right way round. Current is the conductance of the voltage: I = GV, with G = 1/R. Power is the voltage of the current: P = VI. A capacitor is a can: it cans charge, and the charge it cans is the capacitance of its voltage, q = CV. At the start the can holds no charge, so the resistor gets the whole 6.0 V, and the current is the conductance of 6.0 V across 20 Ω: 0.30 A. The time constant is RC = 20 × 0.10 = 2.0 s. When the can's at 4.0 V, the resistor has the other 2.0 V, and the current is 0.10 A. The can holds 0.40 C and ½CV² = 0.80 J, and the resistor's burning the voltage of the current, 2.0 V of 0.10 A, 0.20 W, as heat.
This is a different can, in a separate loop with a coil. A coil coils flux, and the flux it coils is the inductance of its current, Φ = Li.
(2) With C = 1/(Lω_a²), LC = 1/ω_a², so ω₀ = 1/√(LC) = ω_a = 2.42 sweep. The loop on its own cycles with the slow mode exactly.
(3) The statement writes the torque τ_em; here it's χ_em = κi: the current cranks the rod. The power is the rod's sweep of that crank, κiȧ. The statement's minus in ε = −κȧ is a direction: the rod's motion drives an emf of κȧ back against the current that cranks it, so the circuit gives up the voltage of the current, κȧ of i: the same κiȧ. Whatever the rod gets, the circuit gives up. When the current cranks the rod against its sweep, the energy runs the other way, and the rod's giving its energy to the circuit instead. It's a trade: a belt, not a battery. Nothing's made.
(4) (2/3) × 2.42 = 1.61 and (3/2) × 2.42 = 3.63 sweep. Their product is ω₀², 5.86 per second squared, and the faster is 9/4 of the slower: 4 to 9. They're a mirror pair about ω₀: 2/3 and its flip, 3/2. These are the two tones of one machine: rods and loop together.

(3) At T/2 the slow part has made two whole turns and the fast part four turns and a flip. So the slow cosine is back at 1 and the fast one is at −1, and q/q₀ = 9/13 − 4/13 = 5/13. The sines are both zero there, so the current is zero and the upper rod's angle is zero, and with it the lower rod's: the rods pass through vertical. The can holds (5/13)² = 25/169 of 80 mJ, about 12 mJ. With no current the coil holds nothing, and at vertical the rods heft nothing, so the other 68 mJ is the rods' punch. That's 25 and 144 out of 169: a 5–12–13 triangle, the can's share and the rods'.
(4) T is the receiver's own time. In S the same stretch lasts γT = 1.25 × 15.6 = 19.5 s, and the receiver covers 0.60c × 19.5 s = 3.5 × 10⁹ m. Its seat is at x = vt the whole while, so x_H = γ(x − vt) = 0 throughout. The chair stays at x_H = 0 for the whole run. This is RUN: nothing taps the machine, so its two tones lining up adds nothing.

## U1: the machine, all of it
Any of the three trapdoors comes down here.
Take the coupled machine of F2 with its tones at (2/3)ω₀ and (3/2)ω₀, written 4ν and 9ν, so ν = ω₀/6. Scale the swing and the charge as X = √η a and Y = √L q, so that Ẍ = −ω₀²X + ΔωẎ and Ÿ = −ω₀²Y − ΔωẊ, with the gap Δω = 5ν. It starts with Y = Y₀ ≠ 0, X = 0, and Ẋ = Ẏ = 0.
1. Show that the slow mode has X = −B₁ sin 4νt and Y = B₁ cos 4νt, and the fast mode X = B₂ sin 9νt and Y = B₂ cos 9νt.
2. Fit the start and show that B₁ = (9/13)Y₀ and B₂ = (4/13)Y₀.
3. Find the first time the whole state returns, and how the energy is shared between the swing's motion and the capacitor halfway there.
4. Using ω₀² = 10(2 − √2) s⁻² and 80 mJ in the machine, how long is the return?
Solution
(1) Put each trial in. The swing’s balance gives (ω₀² − ω²)A = −Δω ωB and the loop’s gives (ω₀² − ω²)B = −Δω ωA. Thus A = ±B and ω₀² − ω² = ∓Δω ω. In units of ν, 36 − 16 = 20 = 5 × 4 for the slow mode, with A = −B, and 36 − 81 = −45 = −5 × 9 for the fast one, with A = B.
(2) At the start Y = B₁ + B₂ = Y₀, and the swing isn't moving: −4νB₁ + 9νB₂ = 0. So B₂ = (4/9)B₁, B₁ = (9/13)Y₀ and B₂ = (4/13)Y₀.
(3) Both parts come back when 4νT and 9νT are whole turns. Four and nine share no factor, so the first time is νT = τ: four turns low and nine turns high. Halfway, the slow part has made two whole turns and the fast part four and a flip. Then Y = (5/13)Y₀ and Ẋ = −(12/13)ω₀Y₀, so the capacitor holds (5/13)² of the energy and the swing's motion (12/13)²: 25 and 144 out of 169, a 5–12–13 triangle made from 2² and 3².
(4) T = τ/ν = 6τ/ω₀ = 15.6 s. The fixed 80 mJ held through that return gives ∫E dt = 1.2 J s. Here the action counted is that energy-time tally. On the rods’ 2 kg, it’s 0.62 zam. Against the a-swing’s η = 6.8 kg m², the same tally is 0.18 zlam. The reference profile η/(2 kg) takes zlam to zam. Neither tally is energy used up. Nobody asked for either of them.

## U2: TEST, then RUN
On the chassis: a switch with two positions, TEST and RUN, and a small drive arm linked to the mechanical coordinate a.
In TEST the electrical loop is open, so i = 0. The drive acts only on the mechanical coordinate a; the other mechanical mode is absent. Thus X = √η a obeys Ẍ = −ω₀²X between taps. The drive gives N short impulses, each adding the same positive increment to Ẋ. Start from rest, and let E₁ be the energy one tap gives this oscillator from rest.
1. If successive taps are separated by one complete period of this oscillator, show that after N taps its energy is N²E₁, and that the k-th tap does (2k − 1)E₁ of work.
2. If the free phase advances between taps are independent and uniformly distributed over a full cycle, show that the expected energy after N taps is NE₁.
3. Compare the two after 100 taps.
4. In RUN the drive is disconnected and the machine is lossless. Does its energy change when its two tones line up at the return?
Solution
(1) Put ω₀X and Ẋ along the two directions of a state arrow. Its squared length is twice the oscillator’s energy. Between taps the arrow turns with the cycle; each tap adds the same piece along Ẋ. One whole turn between taps brings every addition back into step, so N additions make the arrow N times as long and store N²E₁. The k-th adds k² − (k − 1)² = 2k − 1 units of work. Equal impulses don’t mean equal work: the drive pays more as the motion grows.
(2) Landing at random points, the arrows point every which way. The square of the sum is the sum of the squares plus cross terms, and the cross terms average to zero, so the expected energy is NE₁: a random walk.
(3) A hundred taps in step give a hundred times one tap’s amplitude and 10,000 E₁. Random phases give an expected 100 E₁ and a root-mean-square amplitude ten times one tap’s. The coherent case stores a hundred times the expected energy. The drive supplies the work in either case.
(4) No. In RUN the drive is disconnected and the loop is closed. The machine keeps its 80 mJ; the return puts it all back in the capacitor. The build-up in TEST came from the drive, through its repeated work. Changing the timing can break that step; simply making two free tones line up adds nothing.
