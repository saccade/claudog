## Dining room
## 1. The knife
The loose sheet models the knife as a 1 kg point mass, a meter from its pivot. Let go from 0.1 rad off the downward vertical, it swings under g = 10 m/s². The numbers are on the sheet; the knife has already begun.
(1-a) Leaning through θ lifts a point ℓ away by ℓ(1 − cos θ). For this small lean, the knife rises about ℓ θ²/2: 5 mm. The 10 N weight lifted through 5 mm stores 0.05 J. Energy stored in height is heft: the weight pulls, and the height hefts. The knife hefts 0.05 J.
(1-b) The velocity of added motion is stored as punch: each bit of motion goes in at the velocity the knife has as it arrives. On the arc, the weight's pull along the path and the knife's drop share the same slope, so for each meter of drop the velocity of added velocity is g, wherever the knife is. The square v² takes that in through both of its v’s at once: 2g for each meter. The heft and the punch both carry the same mass, so it cancels:
v² = 2gh.
A 5 mm fall under 10 m/s² gives v² = 0.1 m²/s². The knife passes the bottom at 0.32 m/s.
(1-c) The knife has a cycle: from one end, through the bottom to the other end, and back. It cycles. Call the time for one complete cycle T.
Call a radian a stroke. All the way round is a turn, about 6.3 strokes, and half of that is π, the flip. A flip of the knife’s cycle takes it to the opposite end; a turn brings it back.
Count strokes each second and call that the sweep. The cycle’s sweep is ω. The usual small-swing rule balances as
ℓω² = g,
Tω = 2π.
Here the cycle sweeps at √10, about 3.2 strokes a second. Its cycle takes T = 2π/√10, about two seconds. There's a 2 riding along with that π: two flips standing in for one turn. π only covers half of what the knife does, one swing across; the cycle is the whole of it. Give the whole turn its own sign, τ, and the rule says so:
Tω = τ.
The knife’s own direction sweeps too, at a different pace. At the bottom it sweeps at v/ℓ, about 0.32 strokes a second. At either end that sweep is zero. The knife turns back while the cycle sweeps on. The rate the sweep changes is the pickup: swung θ off vertical, the knife picks up at g/ℓ of its lean, back toward the bottom, θ̈ = −(g/ℓ)θ. A stroke a second makes a handy unit of sweep, so call it a sweep too: the cycle sweeps at 3.2 sweep. At either end the knife picks up at 10 of its 0.1 lean: 1 sweep a second, back toward the bottom.
(1-d) Twice the mass doubles the heft, the punch, and the resistance to being sped up. The height, bottom speed, and time for a cycle stay the same. The cycle never asked for the mass.

## 2. The engine and the flywheel
(2-a) The engine ends each cycle with the same stored energy it began with. Of the 15 J that comes in (heat-in), 3 J comes out as work and the other 12 J leaves (heat-out). Work over heat-in is 3/15: 20%. At a cycle a second, that's 3 W.
(2-b) The sweep of added spin is stored as hook. Built from rest to 4, the spin reaches 2 kg m²/s. The sweep and the spin grow together. Their product grows through both factors at once, by two equal steps, each the size of the bit of hook stored, so it counts the hook twice over: 4 × 2 = 8, so 4 J. Four seconds at 3 W add 12 J, for 16 J. Counted twice over, that’s 32, and at a sweep of 8 the spin is 4 kg m²/s: 8 × 4 = 32. Doubling the sweep doubles both the spin and the sweep at which it was added. Four times the hook buys twice the sweep.
(2-c) Torque usually gets the letter τ, but the turn has it now. A χ hung on the wall could turn something round—use that for torque. Three words sit close together here, each a push on a lever, a newton at a meter, and each with its own job against the arm. Torque turns things around the axle and can do work there. A moment exerts an effort against a load across the arm, and does no work around the axle. Twist acts around the arm itself: the arm is its axis, the way a shaft carries torsion. The engine's χ turns the wheel and does work, so it's torque. Torque's unit is the crank, 1 N m, the way the gram is a unit of mass and the amp a unit of current; a crank is a size of torque, not a new kind of turning. What a torque does is crank, so the verb comes with the unit. The power a torque delivers is the sweep of the torque: P = ωχ. Put numbers to it once: 0.75 crank cranking at 4 sweep delivers 3 W. So at the start the engine cranks the wheel with χ = 0.75 crank, and at the end, sweeping at 8, with 0.375 crank.
When the wheel goes twice as fast, the same power cranks just half as hard. On 500 g m², those torques pick the wheel up at 1.5 sweep a second at the start and 0.75 at the end.
## 3. The governor and the spool

(3-b) The ball hangs g/ω² below the hinge: 10/25 = 0.4 m here. A drop can't be longer than the arm. Below a sweep of √(g/ℓ) = √20, about 4.5 sweep, g/ω² would be longer than the 0.5 m arm, so hanging straight down is all that's left.
(3-c) With the mass taken out, inertia is the profile: I on the spool's 2 kg is a profile of 0.02 m² about the axle. The cord pulls forward (→), and the counter's grip f is taken → too; the answer will say which way it really goes. The center of mass speeds up as if both forces acted on it directly: ma → = F → + f →. Rolling →, the spool turns back around, ↻: the turning carries the bottom back, ↻ × ↓ = ←. Both pulls act below the axle, the cord 0.12 m and the grip 0.2 m, and ↓ × → = ↺, so the cord cranks the spool ↺ about the axle with Fr and the grip cranks it ↺ with fR: Iα ↻ = [Fr + fR] ↺. The two sides carry opposite senses (↻ = −↺), and the numbers say which way it really goes. Rolling ties the pickup to the center: α = a/R. Put together, the ledgers balance as:
(m + I/R²)a = F(1 − r/R).
The profile is 0.02 m², half of R², 0.04 m², so the mass and the turning count as 2 kg at 3/2: 3 kg at the axle, while 2/5 of 6 N gives 2.4 N on the other side. The center speeds up at 0.8 m/s² →: the spool rolls toward the pull, picking up at 4 sweep a second. The grip is what's left when the cord's 6 N → comes off the 1.6 N → the center needs: f = 1.6 N → + 6 N ← = 4.4 N ←. It pulls back, and about the axle it cranks the spool ↻ harder than the cord cranks it ↺. Count the torques in twenty-fifths of a crank. The cord’s 6 N at 3/25 m gives 18 ↺; the grip’s 22/5 N at 1/5 m gives 22 ↻. The grip wins by 4, exactly what 40 g m² needs to pick up at 4 sweep a second.
(3-d) A spool that stays put has a = 0 and α = 0. The cord still leaves the hub at a tangent, 0.12 m from the axle, so it still cranks the spool ↺ with Fr; only its forward part changes, to → · F. With nothing speeding up, the grip is the same size pointing back, → · F · ←, which read from the other end is ← · F · →: pulling 0.2 m below the axle, it cranks the spool ↺ along with the cord, by (← · F) R. The two turnings add, Fr ↺ + (← · F) R ↺, and with nothing turning that's zero. So the forward part is r/R of the pull, 3/5. That's θ = 53°: the 3–4–5 again, with the 3 along the counter. The spool's inertia never comes into it, because a spool that doesn't pick up never asks for it.

## 9. The double pendulum
(9-a) Each mode cycles with its own sweep, and a turn of it takes T = τ/ω. The slow one takes τ/ω_a ≈ 2.6 s and the fast one τ/ω_b ≈ 1.1 s.
(9-b) The squares are in the ratio (2 + √2)/(2 − √2). Multiply top and bottom by 2 + √2 and it's (2 + √2)²/2, so the sweeps are in the ratio (2 + √2)/√2 = 1 + √2. That's irrational, so no whole number of slow cycles ever equals a whole number of fast ones, and a motion with both in it never exactly repeats.
(9-c) The √2 belongs to the rods' angles; here's what it asks of the masses. The lower mass rides on the upper: it's out by the upper rod's swing plus its own. In the slow mode the lower rod leans √2 as far the same way, so the lower mass goes 1 + √2 times as far as the upper, about 2.4. In the fast mode the lower rod leans √2 as far the other way, more than undoing what the upper rod gave it, so the lower mass swings the other way, but only √2 − 1 as far, about 0.41. In either mode, each rod picks up at ω² of its own lean, back toward vertical.
## 10. The coils and the capacitor
(10-a) Say these the right way round. Current is the conductance of the voltage: I = GV, with G = 1/R. Power is the voltage of the current: P = VI. A capacitor is a can: it cans charge, and the charge it cans is the capacitance of its voltage, q = CV. At the start the can holds no charge, so the resistor gets the whole 6 V, and the current is the conductance of 6 V across 20 Ω: 0.3 A. The time constant is RC = 20 × 0.1 = 2 s. When the can's at 4 V, the resistor has the other 2 V, and the current is 0.1 A. The can holds 0.4 C. Voltage and charge grow together, so 4 V of 0.4 C, 1.6 J, counts the can's store twice over: 0.8 J. The resistor's burning the voltage of the current, 2 V of 0.1 A, 0.2 W, as heat.
This is a different can, in a separate loop with a coil. A coil coils flux, and the flux it coils is the inductance of its current, Φ = Li.
(10-b) With C = 1/(Lω_a²), LC = 1/ω_a², so ω₀ = 1/√(LC) = ω_a. The coil and the can give a plain 2.4 per second. A stroke counts as one, so that's 2.4 strokes a second: the loop sweeps with the slow mode exactly.
(10-c) The statement writes the torque τ_em; here it's χ_em = κi: the current cranks the rod. The power is the rod's sweep of that torque, κiȧ. The statement's minus in ε = −κȧ is a direction: the rod's motion drives an emf of κȧ back against the current that cranks it, so the circuit gives up the voltage of the current, κȧ of i: the same κiȧ. Whatever the rod gets, the circuit gives up. When the current cranks the rod against its sweep, the energy runs the other way, and the rod's giving its energy to the circuit instead. The κ is how much of the coil's linked flux comes from where the rod sits: per stroke the rod swings, that part changes by κ, so ε dt = −κ da. The coil's own current links Li on top of that, so in all the coil links Φ = Li + κa, and only the κa part is the rod's doing. So κ does two jobs, torque per amp and flux per stroke, and they're the same unit, a weber per stroke.
(10-d) (2/3)ω₀ ≈ 1.6 and (3/2)ω₀ ≈ 3.6 sweep. Their product is ω₀², 5.9 per second squared, and the faster is 9/4 of the slower: 4 to 9.

## 11. The line

## Drawer f: two crests from two waves
Door: the hall. A hatbox in red and white stripes, rubberized against the wet; inside, a long coil of wire and two tuning forks.
(f-1) cos P + cos Q = 2 cos(½(P − Q)) cos(½(P + Q)) (in arrows, → · P + → · Q = 2 (→ · ½(P − Q)) (→ · ½(P + Q))), with P = k₁x − ω₁t and Q = k₂x + ω₂t.
