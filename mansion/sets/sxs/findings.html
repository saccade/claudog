What two newcomers made of it

Two fresh model readers, each with no context, read the six pairs blind, with A and B shuffled per pair. One read as a high-school junior who has just had a trig unit. The other read as a machinist and crane rigger whose trig is rusty but who knows loads and levers. Neither saw these notes or knew which version was newer.

Where the arrows earned their keep, for both

  • Forces with a sense. "f = 1.6 N → + 6.0 N ← = 4.4 N ←" beat "f = 1.6 − 6.0 = −4.4 N" for both readers. The machinist said it's "how I'd add loads on a sling." The student said the signed version "reads as just arithmetic."
  • Turning senses in a sentence that says which way. The spool, part 4: "it turns the spool ↺ with Fr … the grip … turns the spool ↻ with F (→ · θ) at R. The two balance." The machinist called it "a moment balance, exactly how I'd check a load on a pivot." Both picked the arrow version clearly. The signed version was "pure sign-pushing with no picture."
  • The cross at the cue ball. Both decoded ↑ × → = ↻ from a wrench picture. Both checked ω ↻ × R ↓ = ω R ← themselves. Both called "The two senses agree, so the cue went in above the center" a real check that the scalar version never makes.

Where it didn't

  • Dots of arrows that point the same way. The cue ball's energies, ½ (40 ↻) · (… ↻), read as decoration to both. The machinist: "'each dot is just the product of the sizes' admits it." The chair split them. The student got a picture from it ("two clock hands," → and ←, which explained the 9/13 − 4/13). The machinist called it decoration.
  • Arrows on both sides of an equation. Iα ↻ = [Fr + fR] ↺ stopped both readers. Both guessed that opposite arrows act like a minus sign, with low confidence.
  • The dot standing in for cos, after the first time. The readers split by background:
    • The student, fresh from trig, saw → · θ as "renames cos, and the next step uses it exactly as cos." It never paid for itself in the knife, the governor or the spool.
    • The machinist, rusty on trig, found the knife's version the better one. It showed where 1 − cos comes from: "full arm length minus how far it still hangs."
  • An arrow dotted with an angle. Both balked at "down dot an angle." θ is a number to them, so "↓ · θ" read oddly until they guessed that θ "stands for the way the arm points."

Where words did the work, not symbols

Both preferred the governor's drop and reach, "A drop can't be longer than the arm" over "The cosine can't go over 1," and "the ball hangs g/ω² below the hinge." The student called the ↓ · θ = 0.80 tagged on after that "bolted on." "mg is to mω² of the reach as the drop is to the reach" took both readers two or three reads, then landed.

Turning senses

Both readers' gut said a wheel rolling forward turns forward. Both stumbled at "Rolling →, the spool turns back around, ↻" the first time they met it. The excerpts start partway into the set and carry no sentence naming forward around before it's used.

What both asked for

Each, unprompted, asked for the same thing at a symbol's first use: name it, say what it stands in for, and give a picture. The knife's opening line, "The statement's cos θ is ↓ · θ, the lean's part along straight down," was the one place that did it. Both readers decoded the dot there at once and carried it forward.

Test artifacts, not findings

The governor and spool share one statement, so pieces 2–4 repeat it. The † legend sits at the top of the set, not in these excerpts. Shuffling A and B made the sign style seem to "flip between pieces," which the set itself doesn't do.
